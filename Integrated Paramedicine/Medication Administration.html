1. Routes of Administration & Pharmacokinetics

Rate of Flow & Absorption

The route chosen directly impacts the onset, peak, and duration of the medication's effect. Paramedics must match the route to the clinical urgency.

Route Absorption Profile & Clinical Context
Intravenous (IV) / Intraosseous (IO) Instantaneous absorption (100% bioavailability). Ideal for resuscitation and titratable analgesia. High risk of systemic adverse events (e.g., immediate anaphylaxis, phlebitis).
Intramuscular (IM) Rapid absorption (10-20 mins) via capillary beds. Useful when IV access is difficult or for depot medications (e.g., Adrenaline in anaphylaxis, Glucagon). Variable in shock due to poor peripheral perfusion.
Intranasal (IN) Rapid onset (mucosal absorption avoids hepatic first-pass metabolism). Highly effective for pediatric analgesia (Fentanyl) and sedation (Midazolam). Painless and needle-free.
Inhaled (INH) Rapid local onset (lungs). High surface area for absorption. Ideal for target-specific action (e.g., Salbutamol for bronchospasm) with minimized systemic side effects.
Oral (PO) Slower onset (30-60 mins). Subject to first-pass metabolism in the liver. Requires a conscious, cooperative patient without active vomiting.

Adverse Events Associated with Routes

  • IV/IO: Phlebitis, extravasation (tissue necrosis from vesicant drugs), air embolism, osteomyelitis (IO), rapid systemic toxicity if overdosed.
  • IM: Injection site pain, sterile abscess, nerve injury (e.g., sciatic nerve if gluteal site is improperly targeted).
  • IN: Mucosal irritation, epistaxis, unpredictable absorption if excess mucus or blood is present in the nares.

Ethical Evaluation of Route Selection

Choosing a route is not just a clinical decision; it is an ethical one, balancing four core principles:

  • Need (Clinical Urgency): Does the patient's condition require immediate peak concentration (e.g., IV Adrenaline in cardiac arrest), or is gradual onset appropriate?
  • Risk-Beneficence: Do the benefits of rapid IV access outweigh the risks of delaying treatment to find a vein, or the pain/infection risk of IO placement?
  • Justice: Ensuring equitable care. For example, utilizing Intranasal (IN) pain relief for a distressed pediatric or needle-phobic patient, rather than withholding analgesia because IV access is "too difficult."
  • Sufficiency: Will the chosen route deliver a sufficient therapeutic dose to the target organ? (e.g., IM injections may not reach systemic circulation sufficiently in profound hypovolaemic shock).

2. Cardiac Arrest Pharmacology (ANZCOR Guidelines)

Based on recent ILCOR evidence reviews (2018-2025), the Australian and New Zealand Committee on Resuscitation (ANZCOR) makes explicit recommendations for medication administration during ALS.

Vascular Access Preference

  • IV is Preferred: Intravenous access is the preferred means of administering medications during or after cardiac arrest when compared to IO access.
  • IO as Alternative: If IV access cannot be rapidly achieved within two attempts, it is reasonable to consider IO access.

Adrenaline (Epinephrine) Administration

  • Non-Shockable Rhythms (PEA / Asystole): Recommend 1mg Adrenaline as soon as feasible during CPR, and then every second loop of the ALS algorithm.
  • Shockable Rhythms (VF / pVT): Suggest 1mg Adrenaline after initial defibrillation attempts are unsuccessful (specifically after the 2nd shock) and then every second loop.

Antiarrhythmics & Other Agents

  • Amiodarone: Suggest 300mg for shock-refractory VF/pVT (after the 3rd shock). If still refractory, an additional 150mg may be considered after the 5th shock.
  • Lidocaine (Alternative): Suggest 1mg/kg as an alternative to Amiodarone in shock-refractory VF/pVT (with an additional 0.5mg/kg if still refractory).
  • Fibrinolytics: Should not be used routinely, but may be used when pulmonary embolus is the suspected cause of arrest.
  • Buffers & Electrolytes: Calcium, magnesium, potassium, and sodium bicarbonate may be considered only to manage particular associated conditions (e.g., hyperkalaemia).

Medications Advised AGAINST

  • ANZCOR recommends against routine administration of Calcium for the treatment of OHCA.
  • Suggests against the administration of Vasopressin in place of Adrenaline.
  • Suggests against the addition of Vasopressin to Adrenaline.
  • Suggests against the combination of Vasopressin and Corticosteroids in addition to usual care.

3. Pain Assessment & The Multimodal Assessment Model (MAP)

Pain as a Subjective Experience

Pain is multidimensional, influenced by biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors. According to the Multimodal Assessment Model of Pain (MAP), clinicians face significant challenges in accurately assessing and validating pain:

  • Subjectivity of Pain: Pain is inherently unobservable and deeply personal, making it difficult for clinicians to validate accuracy objectively.
  • Overemphasis on Quantitative Measures: Standardized 1-10 numerical ratings dominate practice. This overlooks the personal context, meaning, and true impact of the pain.
  • Lack of Integration of Qualitative Methods: Vital narrative and behavioral expressions of pain are often under-utilized, missing the idiosyncratic nature of a patient's suffering.
  • Ambiguity in Prioritization: Uncertainty exists when self-reports clash with physiological measures (e.g., a patient rates pain 10/10 but has normal HR/BP and appears calm).
  • Stigma and Validation Issues: Patients feel stigmatized or dismissed when their pain reports lack observable pathology (e.g., chronic pain syndromes like fibromyalgia).
  • Time Constraints: Qualitative pain assessments are often viewed as too burdensome for paramedics dealing with time-critical emergencies.
  • Difficulty with Unexplained Pain: Reports unlinked to clear mechanisms pose diagnostic and management challenges.
  • Cultural Divide in Research: Qualitative methodologies in pain research are often viewed as less generalizable compared to quantitative data.

Promoting Equity in Pain Care

Achieving equity requires recognizing that implicit biases (e.g., racial, gender, or age biases) often lead to the under-treatment of pain in vulnerable populations. Paramedics must employ a holistic approach:

  • Non-Pharmacological: Splinting, cooling/heating, psychological first aid, reassurance, and distraction (especially in pediatrics). These build trust and reduce anxiety-induced pain amplification.
  • Pharmacological: Utilizing multimodal analgesia (combining Paracetamol, NSAIDs, and Opioids) to target different pain pathways, tailoring the route (IV vs IN vs PO) to the patient's individual capability and distress level.

4. Medication Safety (NSQHS Standards)

Standard 4: Medication Safety in Mobile Healthcare

The National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standards mandate rigorous systems to reduce medication errors. In the pre-hospital environment, the absence of automated dispensing cabinets and hospital pharmacists increases the risk of error.

  • Critique of Pre-Hospital Risks: Paramedics draw up potent medications (Opiates, Ketamine, Adrenaline) under extreme cognitive load, poor lighting, and in moving vehicles. Look-alike/sound-alike ampoules exacerbate this risk.
  • Implementation of Safe Practice:
    • The 5 Rights: Right Patient, Right Drug, Right Dose, Right Route, Right Time.
    • Independent Double-Checks: Mandatory cross-checking with a partner before administering Schedule 8 drugs or high-alert infusions.
    • Allergy & History Checking: Rigorous history taking before administration (e.g., confirming Aspirin allergy).

Inter-Professional Strategies for Pharmaceutical Outcomes

Improving medication route selection and outcomes requires cohesive teamwork across the healthcare continuum:

  • Closed-Loop Communication: When an ICP delegates a medication task, the assisting paramedic repeats the order back (e.g., "Drawing up 50mcg of Fentanyl for IV administration").
  • Shared Mental Models: Openly discussing the rationale for a route selection on scene. (e.g., "The patient is poorly perfused, so I'm avoiding IM; let's try for an IV, and if we fail, we'll use IN").
  • Clinical Handover (IMIST-AMBO): Clearly communicating exactly what medications were given, the route, the time, and the patient's response to the receiving ED staff to prevent accidental double-dosing.

Based on ANZCOR Guidelines (Section 11.5) and NSQHS Medication Safety Standards. Always consider the ethical principles of justice and risk-beneficence when determining analgesic interventions and routes of administration.
