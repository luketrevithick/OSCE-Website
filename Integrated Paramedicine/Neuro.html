1. Neurological Assessment Refresh LO1

Why a Systematic Approach Matters

Neurological emergencies — stroke, status epilepticus, raised intracranial pressure (ICP), spinal cord injury, and acute neuromuscular weakness — are time-critical and can deteriorate rapidly. Nonneurologist clinicians (including paramedics) are frequently the first to assess these patients, and prompt recognition directly changes outcomes because most definitive therapies (thrombolysis, aneurysm securing, escalating anti-seizure therapy) are only effective within defined time windows. Your job is not to diagnose the exact lesion — it's to recognise the pattern, protect the brain/cord from secondary injury, and get accurate timing and history to the receiving team.

Clinical pearl: A single-point drop in GCS is clinically significant and should always trigger reassessment of the whole primary survey (hypoxia? hypoglycaemia? expanding bleed?) rather than being logged and left.

1.2 Pupils

Assess size, symmetry, and reactivity to light. As intracranial pressure rises, dysfunction of the ascending reticular activating system causes reduced alertness, and progressive cranial neuropathies — often starting with pupillary abnormalities — accompany brainstem compression as herniation develops.

PatternConsider
Unilateral dilated ("blown") pupilTrauma; compression of cranial nerve III; uncal herniation; posterior communicating artery aneurysm; acute angle-closure glaucoma; local drug contamination (e.g. accidental contact with nebulised salbutamol or ipratropium — a classic mimic worth remembering); tonic (Adie) pupil; optic neuritis.
Bilateral dilated pupilsMidbrain injury; systemic disease (e.g. botulism, Miller Fisher variant of Guillain-Barré); sympathomimetic or anticholinergic drugs/overdose.
Transient dilationSeizure (post-ictal); migraine; unilateral episodic mydriasis.

Paramedic pearl: Before assuming a unilateral dilated pupil means herniation, ask whether the patient (or someone nearby) has recently used a nebuliser — ipratropium mist reaching the eye is a well-recognised, entirely benign cause of a fixed, dilated pupil.

1.3 Rapid Stroke Screen — FAST / BE-FAST

Face (droop) · Arms (drift/weakness) · Speech (slurred, aphasia) · Time (of onset — call it immediately). The extended BE-FAST adds Balance (sudden ataxia/vertigo) and Eyes (sudden vision loss or gaze deviation), which improves detection of posterior circulation strokes that classic FAST can miss.

The NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) is the standardised in-hospital severity score (0 = best, 42 = worst) used to quantify deficit and track evolution — you won't usually complete a full NIHSS on-road, but understanding that the receiving team will use it explains why a clear, structured description of the deficit in your handover (which side, which limbs, speech quality, gaze) is so valuable.

1.4 Stroke Mimics — Always Check a BGL

Not every acute focal deficit is a stroke. Common metabolic derangements can unmask old ischaemic injury or trigger seizures that mimic stroke, so a fingerprick glucose is mandatory before you commit to a "stroke" impression.

1.5 Why Time and History Matter So Much

All hyperacute stroke therapies are only safe and effective within defined windows, so the single most valuable piece of information you can hand over is the confirmed time last seen well (not "time found"). This is why in-hospital eligibility for clot-busting therapy generally requires a clear onset within about 3 hours (extendable to 4.5 hours in selected patients), with hospital teams needing a fast non-contrast CT to exclude bleeding before anything is given. Endovascular clot retrieval can sometimes be considered out to 6 hours or beyond in selected cases.

These treatment decisions happen in hospital — they are included here so you understand why the receiving team will ask you (repeatedly) about onset time, anticoagulant use, recent surgery/trauma, and bleeding history: these are the exclusion criteria that decide whether thrombolysis is even an option.

What is squarely in your scope: accurate onset time, BGL, a structured deficit description, checking for anticoagulant/antiplatelet medications, minimising scene time, and — importantly — not aggressively lowering blood pressure in a suspected ischaemic stroke (permissive hypertension is generally the goal, in contrast to a haemorrhagic stroke where the hospital will actively lower blood pressure). If your service credentials paramedics to treat hypoglycaemia, correct it; otherwise treat the stroke presentation as your default and let the hospital sort out mimics.

This contrast matters clinically because it explains why paramedics should not assume "lower the BP" is always correct in a neuro case — the right approach depends entirely on the (yet undiagnosed) stroke subtype, which is one more reason CT imaging on arrival drives everything.

1.7 Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH)

Suspect SAH with the sudden onset of a severe "thunderclap" headache — often described as the worst headache of the patient's life, reaching maximum intensity within seconds. Most non-traumatic SAH is caused by a ruptured intracranial (berry) aneurysm and carries a mortality approaching 50%. Unlike an intracerebral bleed, blood collects in the CSF spaces around the brain, which can impair CSF drainage and cause hydrocephalus.

Paramedic relevance: a sudden, severe, "worst ever" headache — especially with collapse, vomiting, neck stiffness, or a brief loss of consciousness — should be treated as a time-critical neurosurgical emergency regardless of a normal GCS. Minimise stimulation, treat pain and nausea per protocol, avoid unnecessary exertion/straining, and transport for urgent CT.

1.8 Raised Intracranial Pressure & Herniation

As ICP rises, the brain's ability to autoregulate is lost. Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP = MAP − ICP) falls, and if hypoxia is added on top of poor perfusion, the zone of irreversible injury expands rapidly. Watch for a deteriorating GCS, new pupillary asymmetry, and abnormal posturing (decorticate → decerebrate) as brainstem compression progresses.

Paramedic priorities:
  • Avoid hypoxia at all costs — maintain SpO₂ above 94%.
  • Avoid hyperventilation in routine care — it causes cerebral vasoconstriction and can worsen ischaemia. (Brief, controlled hyperventilation is only used in hospital as a temporising bridge when there are clear signs of active herniation.)
  • Elevate the head of the bed/stretcher to around 30° where spinal precautions and haemodynamics allow, to promote venous drainage.
  • Treat hypotension aggressively — a single drop in blood pressure has been shown to sharply increase TBI mortality; the in-hospital circulation target is a systolic above 110 mmHg.
  • Prevent shivering and hyperthermia; maintain normal body temperature.
  • Rapid, smooth transport with early notification — this patient may need urgent neurosurgical intervention or hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol/hypertonic saline) on arrival.

1.9 Status Epilepticus

Definition: a continuous, unremitting seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes, or recurrent seizures without the patient regaining consciousness in between. Every seizure carries a risk of progressing to status epilepticus, and the longer a seizure continues, the less responsive it becomes to anti-seizure medication — early, assertive treatment prevents neuronal damage and treatment resistance.

Paramedic priorities:
  • ABCs first: positioning to protect the airway, high-flow oxygen, suction as needed. Do not restrain or force anything into the mouth.
  • Time the seizure accurately and note whether it is a first-ever seizure or a known epileptic's seizure that is unusually prolonged.
  • Check BGL — hypoglycaemia is a readily reversible seizure cause and a classic stroke/seizure mimic.
  • Benzodiazepine per your local clinical practice guideline (e.g. midazolam) — early administration is the single most important prehospital intervention.
  • Protect from injury, prepare airway adjuncts, and be ready to support ventilation if post-ictal respiratory depression follows treatment.
  • Transport with a clear handover of seizure duration, number of doses given, and baseline anti-epileptic history.

Hospital context: if benzodiazepines fail, escalation typically follows a staged pathway — a second-line anti-epileptic loading dose (e.g. phenytoin/fosphenytoin), and if seizures still continue or the patient doesn't return to baseline, intubation with continuous sedation and EEG monitoring for refractory status epilepticus. Understanding this staged approach helps you appreciate why your handover of "how many doses, over what time, and response" shapes the next clinical decision within minutes of arrival.

1.10 Spinal Cord Injury — Localising the Level (Non-Traumatic Focus)

Traumatic spinal cord injury mechanisms, immobilisation, and extrication are covered in detail in the Spinal Trauma guide. Here, the focus is on recognising the pattern of cord injury from the history and exam — including non-traumatic causes, which are easy to miss.

This is a time-critical surgical emergency requiring urgent imaging — treat it with the same urgency as a traumatic cord injury.

1.11 Acute Neuromuscular Weakness — Is Ventilation at Risk?

Diseases affecting the peripheral nervous system, neuromuscular junction, or muscle share one dangerous common pathway: progressive weakness of the diaphragm, intercostal, and upper-airway muscles can cause ventilatory failure — sometimes before the patient looks distressed. Treat significant limb weakness of unclear cause as a neurological emergency until proven otherwise.

Paramedic priorities:
  • Ask the patient to count aloud in one breath, or assess whether they can speak in full sentences — a rough bedside proxy for deteriorating vital capacity.
  • Watch closely for bulbar signs: nasal/quiet speech, pooling secretions, weak cough, difficulty swallowing.
  • Keep a low threshold for having airway equipment ready; deterioration can be faster than it looks.
  • Handle gently — autonomic instability in GBS means vital signs can swing unpredictably.
  • Transport to a facility with neurology/ICU capability; these patients often need serial respiratory function measurement and may require IVIG, plasmapheresis, or ventilatory support.

2. Developing a Clinical Placement Learning Contract LO2

What a Learning Contract Is For

A learning contract is a negotiated, written agreement between you, your preceptor, and your university that translates broad unit/course learning outcomes into specific, observable goals for a particular placement block. It exists so that all three parties — student, preceptor, and university — share the same expectations from day one, and so there's a documented basis for feedback and review partway through and at the end of placement.

Generic Learning Contract Framework

Use this structure as a starting scaffold — replace it with your unit's actual template if one is issued, but the underlying logic (goal → link to outcome → strategy → evidence → timeframe → sign-off) is standard across health placement learning contracts.

Learning GoalLinked Outcome / SLOStrategy / ActionEvidence of AchievementTimeframe & Review
Confidently identify and act on advance care planning documentation at a palliative care job. SLO1 (end of life law); SLO4 (law shaping practice) Proactively ask about Resuscitation Plans / Authorised Care Plans on relevant jobs; debrief with preceptor after each palliative case. Preceptor observation notes; reflective log entry; digital storytelling submission. Reviewed at midpoint and final sign-off.
Perform a structured neurological assessment independently and hand over using structured language (FAST/BE-FAST, GCS breakdown, pupils). [Your own goal] [Link to a specific SLO/professional capability domain] [What you will actually do] [How this will be demonstrated/documented] [Dates]

Writing Goals That Actually Work — SMART

  • Specific: "Improve my cannulation" is weak. "Successfully cannulate on the first attempt in at least 3 of 5 attempts, with preceptor sign-off" is usable.
  • Measurable: tie the goal to something a preceptor can actually observe and comment on (a number of attempts, a documented conversation, a completed handover).
  • Achievable: scaled to your current stage — a first placement goal should look different from a final-year goal.
  • Relevant: explicitly linked back to a unit SLO or a Paramedicine Board professional capability domain (see Section 3 below) — this is what makes the contract defensible at review time.
  • Time-bound: a stated review point (midpoint, final day) so drift doesn't go unnoticed.

Suggested Process

  1. Before placement: self-assess honestly against the professional capability domains (Section 3) and this unit's specific learning outcomes. Identify 2–3 genuine growth areas — not just things you're already good at.
  2. Day one: negotiate the contract with your preceptor. This is a two-way conversation — your preceptor may see gaps or opportunities you haven't considered.
  3. Midpoint: formal check-in against each goal; adjust strategies if something isn't working rather than waiting until the end to discover it.
  4. Final review: preceptor sign-off with specific evidence against each goal, feeding into your overall placement assessment.

3. Help-Seeking Procedure & Preceptor Reporting Domains LO3

A Generic Escalation Pathway

Every service and university has its own specific placement escalation policy — check your handbook for exact names and contact details — but almost all follow the same underlying tiered logic:

TierWhoWhen to Use
1Your on-road preceptor/supervising clinicianImmediate clinical questions, in-the-moment safety concerns, day-to-day feedback.
2Clinical placement facilitator / unit coordinatorPlacement logistics, unresolved disagreements with a preceptor, concerns about the quality or pace of learning.
3Course coordinator / clinical education team / service clinical support lineUnresolved issues after Tier 2, patterns across multiple shifts, or a critical incident requiring formal review.
4University student support / counselling servicesPersonal wellbeing, stress, or mental health impacts arising from placement.

Escalate Immediately, Don't Wait, If...

  • You are asked to do something outside your scope of practice or that you believe is unsafe for the patient.
  • You experience a needlestick injury, exposure, or any workplace injury.
  • You witness or are subject to harassment, discrimination, or bullying.
  • A critical incident occurs (serious adverse patient outcome, major MVC, line-of-duty exposure).
  • You feel your own physical or psychological safety is at risk.

Routine developmental feedback (skills practice, pacing of learning, timetable clashes) can generally go through Tier 1–2 in the normal course of placement rather than as an urgent escalation.

Preceptor Reporting Domains — Paramedicine Board of Australia

Whatever the exact wording of your university's preceptor report form, Australian paramedicine accreditation standards require every course to map its learning outcomes back to the Professional capabilities for registered paramedics, published by the Paramedicine Board of Australia. These are grouped into five domains — this is very likely the underlying framework your preceptor's report is assessing you against, even if the form uses its own headings:

Knowing these five domains in advance helps you understand why your preceptor might be watching for things beyond raw clinical skill — communication style, documentation habits, and how you respond to feedback all map directly onto this framework and onto your eventual registration standard.

4. Applying eMIMS in Clinical Cases LO4

What eMIMS Is

eMIMS (now published as eMIMSplus/eMIMSelite) is the electronic edition of MIMS — Australia's most widely used independent medicines reference, in continuous publication since 1963. It provides up-to-date Australian Product Information and Consumer Medicine Information for every TGA-registered medicine, is reviewed and updated monthly, and is recommended by professional bodies including the Pharmacy Board of Australia. It's used across Australian healthcare — including by paramedics and paramedicine students — as a point-of-care decision-support tool.

What You Can Look Up

  • Indications & dosing for a medicine the patient is already taking (or that you're considering administering).
  • Contraindications and precautions — including drug-disease contraindications relevant to the presenting complaint.
  • Drug interaction checker — flags potentially harmful combinations across a patient's whole medication list.
  • Allergy cross-checking.
  • Pregnancy category — Australia uses an A/B1/B2/B3/C/D/X categorisation system for medicine safety in pregnancy.
  • Pill identifier — useful when a patient (or bystander) presents with unlabelled medication and you need to identify what's actually been taken.
  • PBS restrictions/pricing — less relevant prehospital, but useful context for understanding why a patient may not be taking a medicine as prescribed.

A Worked Process for Applying eMIMS to a Case

  1. Collect a complete medication list from the patient, their dosette box, or a bystander/carer — brand and generic names, doses, and timing.
  2. Search eMIMS for any medication you don't immediately recognise, to confirm class, indication, and typical dosing.
  3. Run the interaction checker across the patient's full medication list — not just the one drug you're focused on.
  4. Check for contraindications specific to what you are considering administering (e.g. a drug that prolongs the QT interval, in a patient already on another QT-prolonging medicine).
  5. If relevant, check pregnancy category for a person of reproductive age before administering anything beyond first-line, well-established prehospital drugs.
  6. Document what you found and relay it clearly in your handover — this is often the single most useful piece of medication-reconciliation information the receiving team gets.

Worked Example 1 — Anticoagulated Head Strike

An 82-year-old on apixaban for atrial fibrillation has struck their head in a fall. A quick eMIMS check confirms apixaban is a direct factor Xa inhibitor, dosed twice daily, with no rapid at-home reversal available and a specific reversal agent (andexanet alfa) that exists but is a hospital-only, specialist-initiated therapy. This doesn't change your on-road treatment, but it sharpens why this "alert and talking" patient with a normal GCS is still a time-critical transport: any intracranial bleeding in an anticoagulated patient can expand rapidly, and the receiving team needs to know the specific agent and last dose time immediately on arrival to plan reversal and imaging.

Worked Example 2 — Analgesia in a Reproductive-Age Patient

A 28-year-old with severe renal colic-type pain is a candidate for an NSAID and/or opioid analgesia under your clinical practice guidelines. Before administering, a quick pregnancy-category check (and a direct question about the possibility of pregnancy) is good practice — some analgesics carry specific pregnancy-category cautions, particularly in later trimesters. eMIMS gives you the categorisation and rationale in seconds so you can make an informed, protocol-consistent decision and clearly document your reasoning.

Scope reminder: eMIMS is a decision-support and verification tool — it does not replace, expand, or override your service's authorised clinical practice guidelines and drug protocols. Use it to check and cross-reference, not to justify administering something outside your credentialed scope.

5. Appraising the Clinical Placement Digital Storytelling Template LO5

What Digital Storytelling Is For in a Placement Context

Digital storytelling is a short, multimodal reflective piece — typically built in a tool like Adobe Express — that combines narration, text, and images (sometimes music) into a brief narrative about a real placement experience. In health education, it's used to consolidate tacit, in-the-moment learning that's hard to capture in a standard written reflection, and to build communication and self-presentation skills that transfer directly into patient and family communication.

A Generic Appraisal Framework

When you sit down to appraise (or use) a digital storytelling template, run it against these criteria:

CriterionWhat to Look For
Narrative structureDoes the template scaffold a clear beginning–middle–end, ideally anchored to a recognised reflective model (e.g. Gibbs' Reflective Cycle, or Driscoll's "What? So What? Now What?")?
Alignment to learning outcomesDoes it explicitly prompt reflection tied to the unit's SLOs (here, SLO1 and SLO4 — end of life law and its effect on practice), rather than a generic "tell us about your day"?
Confidentiality & privacyDoes the template build in prompts to de-identify patients, avoid any identifying images/audio, and comply with the Paramedicine Board's Code of Conduct on patient privacy? This is non-negotiable in a healthcare reflective assessment.
Multimodal literacyIs the blend of narration, text, and imagery purposeful rather than decorative? Are captions/accessibility features available?
Assessment alignmentIs a marking rubric visible, and does the template's structure actually let you demonstrate each rubric criterion?
Technical usabilityExport format, length limits, and file size — does the template make it easy to produce something that actually submits cleanly within those constraints?

Suggested Production Process

  1. Select a moment from placement with genuine learning value — often the cases that felt uncomfortable or uncertain (a palliative call where the law's presence or absence shaped what you could do is a strong candidate, given SLO4).
  2. Storyboard using a reflective model before touching the software — what happened, what you felt, what you learned, what you'd do differently.
  3. Gather or create fully de-identified visuals — stock imagery, illustrations, or your own non-identifying photos of equipment/environment, never patients.
  4. Record narration in your own voice where the assessment calls for it — authenticity matters more than production polish.
  5. Check against the rubric and confidentiality requirements before finalising.
  6. Export and submit in the required format.

About the ELLC Program

It comprises 13 self-paced online modules covering the law relating to end of life decision-making across every Australian state and territory, and is CPD-accredited for multiple professional colleges. Deep Dive: The Concepts Most Relevant to Paramedics

Consent & Capacity (Module 2)

Valid consent must be voluntary, specific to the treatment proposed, and given by a person with capacity for that particular decision at that particular time. Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific — a person can lack capacity for one decision while retaining it for another, and capacity can fluctuate (e.g. with pain, intoxication, delirium, or fatigue). Adults are presumed to have capacity unless there's reason to doubt it.

Advance Care Directives (Module 4)

An Advance Care Directive (ACD) is a document made by a person with capacity, setting out their wishes or specific instructions about future medical treatment, for use once they lose capacity to decide for themselves. A valid, applicable ACD that clearly refuses a specific treatment generally must be followed — even in an emergency — because it operates as the person's own contemporaneous refusal of consent, made while they could still decide. In NSW this may take the form of a written NSW Health Advance Care Directive, or another document/verbal instruction that meets the legal requirements for validity.

Paramedic reality check: the challenge is rarely the law itself — it's whether a valid, applicable, and current ACD is actually available, legible, and clearly on-point for the specific situation at 3am on scene.

Substitute Decision-Makers (Module 5)

When a person lacks capacity and has no applicable ACD, someone else must be consulted. In NSW, the "person responsible" is identified through a hierarchy under guardianship legislation: first, any guardian (including an Enduring Guardian the person appointed while they had capacity) if the decision falls within that guardian's authorised functions; then a spouse or de facto partner with a close, continuing relationship; then an unpaid carer providing regular support; then a close friend or relative. This hierarchy exists precisely so paramedics and clinicians aren't left guessing who has authority to speak for the patient.

The Doctrine of Double Effect (Module 6)

Providing appropriately titrated pain and symptom relief is lawful even if, as a foreseen but unintended side effect, it may hasten death — provided the primary intention is genuinely to relieve suffering and the dose is proportionate to that goal. This is the legal principle that distinguishes ordinary, appropriate palliative symptom management from voluntary assisted dying, where causing death is the intended outcome. Understanding this distinction matters because fear of legal liability (sometimes called "opiophobia") can otherwise lead clinicians to under-treat a dying patient's pain.

Urgent (Emergency) Treatment for Adults (Module 9)

This is the module most directly relevant to prehospital practice. The law generally permits necessary life-sustaining treatment to be given without consent in a genuine emergency, when the person lacks capacity and there's no reasonably available substitute decision-maker or applicable directive to consult in time. This permission is not, however, unlimited: if a valid, applicable ACD refusing that specific treatment is known to exist, it should still be respected even in an emergency, because the person has already exercised their right to refuse in advance.

Futile or Non-Beneficial Treatment (Module 8)

Treatment can lawfully be withheld or withdrawn where it would not provide any overall benefit to the patient — this determination sits with treating clinicians (usually in consultation with family/substitute decision-makers), not with the family alone. In the prehospital environment, paramedics rarely make this determination independently; this is precisely the situation an Authorised Palliative Care Plan or Resuscitation Plan (Section 7) is designed to pre-empt, by documenting the treating team's decision in advance for paramedics to act on.

Managing Conflict (Module 10)

When family members disagree with each other, with the patient's documented wishes, or with the treating team about end of life decisions, formal dispute-resolution pathways exist through guardianship tribunals (in NSW, the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal — NCAT) and courts. Paramedics are not expected to resolve these disputes on scene — the priority is safe, immediate patient care plus clear documentation and escalation, leaving formal resolution to the appropriate body.

Voluntary Assisted Dying (Module 11)

Voluntary assisted dying (VAD) is a lawful practice — separate from, and legally distinct from, appropriate symptom relief or withholding/withdrawing treatment — in which an eligible person with a terminal illness requests and receives medication to end their life, subject to strict eligibility criteria and process safeguards. As of September 2026, VAD is now law in every Australian state and territory: Victoria (since 2019), Western Australia (2021), Tasmania (2022), Queensland and South Australia (2023), New South Wales (2023), and the ACT (November 2025). The Northern Territory passed its own VAD legislation on 27 August 2026, but it will not be operational until early 2028 while implementation systems are built. Each jurisdiction has its own eligibility criteria (e.g. a prognosis of death within a defined timeframe in most states, with no such timeframe in the ACT) and residency requirements, and Commonwealth law currently restricts the use of telehealth to discuss or arrange VAD.

Scope note: Paramedics are not involved in the assessment, prescription, or administration of VAD medication — this sits entirely within the specific medical/nurse practitioner pathway set out in each state's Act. What is relevant to paramedic practice is recognising the difference between a person who has legally accessed VAD (where death was an intended, planned outcome under a specific legislative scheme) and any other end of life presentation, and following your service's specific policy if called to a scene involving VAD.

Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander Peoples & End of Life Law (Module 12)

Culturally safe end of life care requires understanding that decision-making at the end of life may involve extended family and community/kinship structures beyond the legal "person responsible" hierarchy, and that culturally appropriate practices around death, dying, and the body matter deeply. Cultural safety is determined by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander individuals, families, and communities themselves — it requires ongoing critical reflection by the practitioner on their own knowledge, biases, and the power differentials present in the clinical encounter, and care that is free from racism.

Inclusive Care for Diverse Populations (Module 13)

The same legal principles apply universally, but their practical application needs adjustment for different populations: recognising a same-sex partner or chosen family member's role as a substitute decision-maker for LGBTIQ+ patients; engaging professional interpreters (not family members) for people from culturally and linguistically diverse or refugee backgrounds; ensuring people with disability are supported (not bypassed) in expressing their own wishes wherever they have capacity to do so; and recognising that frailty alone does not equate to a loss of capacity or a reduced entitlement to full, respectful decision-making involvement.

7. Affordances & Limitations: How the Law Shapes Paramedic Practice and the Palliative Care Experience LO7 · SLO4

A Framework for Thinking About This

An affordance is something the law enables or makes possible — it gives paramedics a tool, a permission, or a clear answer. A limitation is where the same legal framework falls short in the messy reality of an out-of-hospital emergency — where it's silent, inaccessible, poorly matched to the situation, or creates a bias toward a default action that may not reflect what the dying person actually wanted. Almost every legal mechanism in end of life care has both faces at once.

Worked Examples: Affordance vs Limitation

Legal MechanismAffordanceLimitation
Advance Care Directives Gives a patient a documented voice when they can no longer speak for themselves, and gives paramedics confidence to honour those wishes. Often not present, not accessible, out of date, or not specific enough to the exact situation unfolding — leaving paramedics to interpret intent under time pressure.
NSW Ambulance Authorised Palliative Care Plans (APCP) & NSW Health Resuscitation Plans Translate a treating doctor's decisions into an explicit, paramedic-specific set of instructions — resuscitation status, preferred place of care, symptom-control medication orders — reducing unwanted transport or resuscitation. Require prior completion and formal endorsement (historically taking weeks to process), aren't accessible for a patient who deteriorates suddenly before one is arranged, and are only valid Monday–Friday processing windows for new/updated plans — a real gap for weekend or after-hours crises.
Emergency treatment doctrine (Module 9) Allows paramedics to act decisively to save life without consent when it's genuinely needed and no other guidance is available. Creates a structural bias toward resuscitating/transporting by default, which can work against a dying person's likely wishes when those wishes simply weren't captured in a legally recognised document on scene.
Doctrine of double effect Gives clear legal protection for proportionate, well-intentioned symptom relief, even near the end of life. Some clinicians remain cautious about litigation risk and under-treat pain as a result ("opiophobia") — the legal protection exists, but confidence in using it doesn't always follow.
Substitute decision-maker hierarchy Provides a clear default answer for who speaks for the patient when no ACD exists. Verifying relationship status and priority within the hierarchy at short notice on scene is genuinely difficult, and family members of equal standing can disagree with each other in the moment.
State-based legal variation (including VAD) Allows each jurisdiction to tailor its framework to local needs and community views. Creates real uncertainty for paramedics attending patients who have recently moved between states, or for services operating near state borders, since ACD formats, guardianship rules, and VAD eligibility all differ.

Practical Strategies for Developing Your Own Practice

  • Ask early and directly on any palliative or clearly life-limiting-illness job whether a Resuscitation Plan, Authorised Care Plan, or Advance Care Directive exists — don't wait for the family to volunteer it.
  • When documentation is absent or ambiguous, use your service's clinical support/medical advice line to get real-time guidance rather than guessing under pressure.
  • Document your reasoning thoroughly, not just your actions — in end of life cases, why you did (or didn't do) something is often as clinically and legally important as what you did.
  • Debrief after difficult end of life calls — both for your own wellbeing and to build a genuine, reflective understanding of how the law played out in that specific case.
  • Stay current: this is one of the fastest-moving areas of Australian health law (VAD alone has changed in every jurisdiction within the last seven years) — what you learn this year should be treated as a snapshot, not a permanent reference.
