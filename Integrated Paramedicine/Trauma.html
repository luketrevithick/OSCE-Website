Why Are You Learning This?

Learning Outcomes

  • Demonstrate that mechanism of injury and patient history are fundamental information to anticipate the care required for potential/associated injuries and their severity.
  • Recognise the need to prioritise treatment toward reversing hypothermia, hypotension (including internal and external haemorrhage) and acidosis (respiratory or metabolic).
  • Provide time-critical and judicious treatments for blunt and penetrating trauma whilst enabling efficient use of resources, relevant referral and early assistance pathways.

Contributes to SLO1–6.

Mechanism of Injury & Patient History (Learning Outcome 1)

Why Mechanism of Injury (MOI) Matters

MOI describes how energy was transferred to the body. The amount, direction and rate of energy transfer predicts the pattern and severity of underlying injury, often before it is clinically obvious. A high index of suspicion built from the MOI and history allows the paramedic to anticipate injuries that have not yet declared themselves — rather than waiting for signs of decompensation.

Kinematics – Common Patterns

History as a Force Multiplier

Bystander, patient, and scene information (speed, restraint use, fall height, weapon type, position found) should shape the primary survey before the patient is even reached. Vehicle telemetry data (crash pulse, delta-V) is increasingly used to refine this picture. History does not replace hands-on assessment, but it tells you where to look hardest.

Predictive Factors for Serious Injury & Field Triage

Mechanism-Based Predictors of Serious Injury

  • Death of another occupant in the same vehicle
  • Ejection from the vehicle
  • Entrapment requiring extrication
  • Lack of seat belt use
  • High speed impact (>88.5 km/hr)
  • Falls from height
  • Axial load or diving injuries
  • Adverse vehicle telemetry crash variables (e.g. high delta-V)

Special Considerations Affecting Triage

  • Older age: reduced physiological reserve — normal vital signs can mask serious injury.
  • Cardiopulmonary or neurologic co-morbidities: blunt the compensatory response to shock and complicate assessment.
  • Anticoagulant use: markedly increases bleeding risk from mechanisms that would otherwise be considered minor.

Limitations of the Evidence

  • Heterogeneity in study designs, definitions of predictive measures, and outcomes across the trauma literature.
  • Most supporting studies are retrospective and rely on large trauma registries, introducing selection and reporting bias.
  • Variability between studies in age cutoffs and in what counts as "serious injury" makes direct comparison difficult.

Conclusions for Practice

  • Certain mechanism and special-consideration criteria are genuinely useful for field triage — high-speed collisions, falls from height, and axial load injuries in particular.
  • EMS provider clinical judgment improves under-triage rates but can increase over-triage — a trade-off, not a flaw, of the "if in doubt, triage up" principle.
  • Future Field Triage Guideline (FTG) revisions should consider adding high speed (>88.5 km/hr), extrication, and specific comorbidities as explicit criteria, alongside standardising age cutoffs and injury definitions.

The Lethal Triad: Hypothermia, Hypotension & Acidosis (Learning Outcome 2)

A Self-Perpetuating Cycle

Haemorrhage (internal or external) causes hypotension and tissue hypoperfusion. Poorly perfused tissue shifts to anaerobic metabolism, producing lactate and driving metabolic acidosis; inadequate ventilation adds a respiratory component. Heat loss — from exposed skin, cold fluids, and a cold environment — causes hypothermia, which impairs clotting enzyme function. Acidosis further impairs clot formation. The resulting coagulopathy worsens haemorrhage, deepening hypotension — closing a vicious, self-perpetuating cycle that becomes progressively harder to break the longer it runs.

The Cycle at a Glance

ElementPrehospital CauseConsequence
Hypotension / HaemorrhageExternal bleeding, internal bleeding (chest, abdomen, pelvis, long bones)Tissue hypoperfusion, anaerobic metabolism
AcidosisAnaerobic metabolism (metabolic) ± hypoventilation (respiratory)Impaired clotting enzyme function
HypothermiaExposure, cold IV fluids, immobility, wet clothingImpaired platelet function & clotting cascade

Priorities: Break the Cycle Early

  • Control haemorrhage first: direct pressure, wound packing, tourniquets for compressible external bleeding, pelvic binder for suspected pelvic fracture.
  • Haemostatic / permissive hypotension resuscitation: titrate fluids to a palpable radial pulse rather than a "normal" blood pressure in uncontrolled haemorrhage, to avoid dislodging early clot.
  • Prevent and treat hypothermia: remove wet clothing, cover the patient, use warmed IV fluids where available, minimise on-scene exposure time.
  • Correct acidosis indirectly: optimise oxygenation and ventilation, control haemorrhage promptly, and minimise scene time to expedite definitive haemorrhage control.
  • Treat all three elements together — reversing only one while ignoring the others allows the cycle to continue.

Blunt vs Penetrating Trauma (Learning Outcome 3)

FeatureBlunt TraumaPenetrating Trauma
MechanismCompression, shear, deceleration forcesDirect tissue disruption along a wound tract ± temporary cavitation (high-velocity)
Injury visibilityOften hidden — significant internal injury with minimal external signsEntry (and possible exit) wound visible, but internal extent often underestimated
Typical injuriesSolid organ injury, closed fractures, diffuse axonal injury, aortic injuryVascular injury, pneumothorax/haemothorax, hollow viscus injury, injury to any structure in the wound path
Field priorityHigh index of suspicion from MOI; careful serial assessmentRapid haemorrhage control; assume the worst along the tract

Time-Critical, Judicious Treatment Principles

  • Minimise scene time for time-critical patients — perform only interventions that change outcome before transport (haemorrhage control, airway, decompression of tension pneumothorax); package and go.
  • Chest seal / needle decompression for penetrating chest trauma with respiratory distress or suspected tension pneumothorax.
  • Judicious analgesia and spinal care guided by mechanism and clinical findings rather than mechanism alone (see Spinal Trauma guide for clearance tools).
  • Match the intervention to the threat: not every patient needs every procedure — resources and scene time are finite and should be spent where they change outcome.
  • Penetrating trauma with uncontrolled haemorrhage and blunt trauma with the lethal triad both benefit from the same underlying principle: stop the bleeding, keep the patient warm, and get to definitive care fast.

Time-Critical Management, Resources & Referral Pathways

Primary Survey Priorities (<C>ABCDE)

  1. <C> Catastrophic haemorrhage: control immediately (tourniquet/direct pressure) before anything else.
  2. A – Airway with cervical spine consideration where indicated by mechanism/findings.
  3. B – Breathing: identify and treat immediately life-threatening chest injuries.
  4. C – Circulation: ongoing haemorrhage control, judicious fluid resuscitation, pelvic binder if indicated.
  5. D – Disability: rapid neurological assessment (GCS, pupils, limb movement).
  6. E – Exposure: full assessment balanced against active prevention of hypothermia.

Efficient Use of Resources & Early Referral

  • Early pre-notification / trauma call: allows the receiving facility to mobilise the trauma team before arrival.
  • Right patient, right hospital: transport time-critical trauma to a facility with appropriate surgical/trauma capability, bypassing closer but lower-capability facilities where protocols support this.
  • Retrieval / aeromedical support: escalate early for rural, entrapped, or physiologically unstable patients where road transport time is prolonged.
  • Calibrate resource intensity to injury severity: avoid over-triage of minor mechanisms and under-triage of high-risk mechanisms — both carry system and patient costs.

Special Populations

Older Patients

Reduced physiological reserve means normal or near-normal vital signs can be present despite significant injury or shock. Maintain a high index of suspicion from mechanism alone, and triage up rather than reassure down.

Patients on Anticoagulants

Even apparently minor trauma (e.g. a ground-level fall with head strike) carries significant risk of serious haemorrhage, particularly intracranial. A low threshold for transport and early disclosure of anticoagulant use to the receiving team is essential.

Cardiopulmonary or Neurologic Co-morbidities

Pre-existing disease can blunt the normal compensatory response to hypovolaemia (e.g. beta-blockers preventing a tachycardic response) or mask neurological deterioration. Interpret vital signs and GCS in the context of the patient's baseline where known.

Reflective Practice

Consider these clinical scenarios:

  1. An unrestrained driver involved in a high-speed frontal collision with a fatality in the same vehicle, but who appears well on your arrival — how does MOI change your management even with reassuring vital signs?
  2. An older patient on anticoagulants after a low-speed fall — how does this change your threshold for transport and destination?
  3. A patient with a penetrating chest wound and uncontrolled external haemorrhage — how do you sequence catastrophic haemorrhage control, chest management, and scene time?
  4. A trapped, hypothermic patient with prolonged extrication time — how would you address all three arms of the lethal triad simultaneously rather than sequentially?

This guide draws on prehospital trauma triage evidence regarding predictive factors for serious injury, and on current understanding of the lethal triad of trauma (hypothermia, hypotension/haemorrhage, and acidosis). Always follow local NSW Ambulance trauma protocols and apply clinical judgment — mechanism and history anticipate injury; hands-on assessment confirms it.
