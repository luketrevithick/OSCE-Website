How to read the dosing tables

Every indication now has (1) an expanded pathophysiology with a stepwise cascade, (2) a drug table that separates the mechanism of action from the mechanism of reversal (how that action undoes the disease process), and (3) a dose, repeat and maximum table.

NSWA-sourced* doses come from NSW Ambulance-derived material (NSW Ambulance published reports and CPG-based study material) but the CPG app itself could not be read during compilation. ECAT / ANZCOR doses come from NSW Health Emergency Care Institute protocols or the Australian and New Zealand Committee on Resuscitation and are not necessarily the paramedic dose. Verify in CPG means no reliable NSWA figure was found.

Always confirm every dose against the current NSW Ambulance CPG app before clinical use or assessment. Authorisation by level (P1, ALS, ICP) also varies; ICP-level drugs are labelled where known.

Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)

Pathophysiology

ACS is a spectrum (unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI) produced by acute myocardial ischaemia, i.e. a mismatch between myocardial oxygen supply and demand. The usual trigger is rupture or erosion of a lipid-rich atherosclerotic plaque with a thin fibrous cap. Rupture exposes sub-endothelial collagen, von Willebrand factor and tissue factor to flowing blood.

Platelets adhere (GP Ib/IX binding vWF), are activated, and release thromboxane A2 (TXA2) and ADP, which recruit more platelets and activate the GP IIb/IIIa receptor so platelets cross-link via fibrinogen. Tissue factor simultaneously drives the coagulation cascade to thrombin, which converts fibrinogen to a fibrin mesh. A platelet-fibrin thrombus that partially occludes the artery gives unstable angina or NSTEMI; complete occlusion gives STEMI. Coronary vasospasm and endothelial dysfunction add a dynamic component.

Downstream, the myocyte switches to anaerobic metabolism: ATP falls, lactate and H+ accumulate, the Na+/K+-ATPase fails and intracellular Ca2+ overloads. Ischaemic nociceptors fire (adenosine, bradykinin, H+), causing pain, and the sympathetic surge raises heart rate and blood pressure, which increases oxygen demand further. Irreversible necrosis spreads as a wavefront from endocardium to epicardium over roughly 20-40 minutes onwards, and the unstable electrical environment risks VF.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Plaque rupture / erosion exposes collagen, vWF and tissue factor
  2. Platelet adhesion, activation, TXA2 and ADP release, GP IIb/IIIa cross-linking
  3. Thrombin generation and fibrin mesh stabilise the clot
  4. Coronary flow falls: supply-demand mismatch, anaerobic metabolism, pain
  5. Sympathetic surge raises HR/BP, raising demand further
  6. Myocyte necrosis, arrhythmia (VF), pump failure

Adrenal Crisis (Acute Adrenal Insufficiency)

Pathophysiology

Cortisol (glucocorticoid) and aldosterone (mineralocorticoid) are essential for stress survival. Cortisol is permissive for catecholamine action (it maintains adrenergic receptor expression and vascular tone), drives hepatic gluconeogenesis, supports glycogen stores, and restrains inflammation. Aldosterone promotes Na+ and water reabsorption and K+/H+ excretion in the distal nephron.

In primary insufficiency (Addison's disease, usually autoimmune) both hormones are lost. In secondary/tertiary insufficiency (most commonly long-term exogenous steroids suppressing the HPA axis) cortisol is lost but aldosterone, controlled by the renin-angiotensin system, is largely preserved. A physiological stressor (sepsis, trauma, surgery, vomiting causing missed doses) raises cortisol demand by several-fold; without the response, crisis follows.

The result is vasodilatory shock that is poorly responsive to fluids and vasopressors (loss of permissive catecholamine effect), plus hypovolaemia from salt wasting, hypoglycaemia (impaired gluconeogenesis), hyponatraemia, and in primary disease hyperkalaemia. Patients present with severe abdominal pain, vomiting, weakness, confusion, hypotension and collapse.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Stressor with inadequate cortisol (missed dose, illness, trauma, abrupt steroid cessation)
  2. Loss of permissive effect: vasodilation, reduced catecholamine responsiveness
  3. Loss of aldosterone (primary disease): Na+ and water wasting, K+ retention
  4. Impaired gluconeogenesis: hypoglycaemia
  5. Hypovolaemic plus distributive shock, confusion, collapse

Allergic Reaction (Mild to Moderate)

Pathophysiology

This is a Type I (IgE-mediated) hypersensitivity. On first exposure, allergen presentation drives Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) that switch B cells to produce allergen-specific IgE, which binds Fc-epsilon-RI receptors on mast cells and basophils (sensitisation). On re-exposure, allergen cross-links surface IgE and triggers degranulation within minutes.

Pre-formed mediators (histamine, tryptase) are released immediately; newly synthesised mediators (leukotrienes C4/D4/E4, prostaglandin D2, PAF) follow. Histamine at H1 receptors causes vasodilation (erythema, warmth), increased permeability of post-capillary venules (wheals, angioedema) and stimulation of sensory C-fibres (itch). A late-phase reaction 4-8 hours later is driven by recruited eosinophils.

By definition the reaction is confined to skin and mucosa (urticaria, itch, mild swelling, flushing) without respiratory or cardiovascular compromise. If wheeze, stridor, hypotension or persistent GI symptoms develop it is anaphylaxis and needs adrenaline.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells and basophils
  2. Degranulation: histamine, tryptase, then leukotrienes and PGD2
  3. H1 receptor activation: vasodilation, venular leak, itch
  4. Localised urticaria, angioedema, erythema
  5. Escalation to systemic features = anaphylaxis

Analgesia (Pain Relief)

Pathophysiology

Pain is processed in four steps. Transduction: tissue injury releases bradykinin, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), H+, ATP and cytokines that activate and sensitise nociceptors (TRPV1, Nav1.7/1.8 channels). Transmission: fast myelinated A-delta fibres (sharp pain) and slow unmyelinated C fibres (dull, burning pain) synapse in dorsal horn laminae I/II, releasing glutamate and substance P onto AMPA and NMDA receptors; second-order neurons ascend in the spinothalamic tract to thalamus and cortex. Perception: sensory cortex (location, intensity) and limbic cortex (distress). Modulation: descending inhibition from the periaqueductal grey and rostral ventromedial medulla (serotonin, noradrenaline, endogenous opioids) dampens dorsal horn transmission.

Peripheral sensitisation (PGE2 lowers nociceptor threshold) causes hyperalgesia at the injury. With sustained input, central sensitisation develops: repeated C-fibre firing relieves the Mg2+ block of NMDA receptors ('wind-up'), amplifying and prolonging pain. Untreated pain also drives sympathetic activation (tachycardia, hypertension, increased O2 demand), hypoventilation from splinting and, in trauma, a stress response that worsens outcomes.

Choice of drug follows the site of action: peripheral (NSAID), central non-opioid (paracetamol), inhaled CNS depressant (methoxyflurane), opioid receptor agonists (morphine, fentanyl) and NMDA antagonism (ketamine) for severe or neuropathic/sensitised pain.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Tissue injury: PGE2, bradykinin, H+ sensitise nociceptors
  2. A-delta/C fibre input to dorsal horn (glutamate, substance P)
  3. Wind-up via NMDA receptors: central sensitisation
  4. Spinothalamic ascent: thalamus, sensory and limbic cortex
  5. Sympathetic response, splinting, distress; descending inhibition may be overwhelmed

Anaphylaxis

Pathophysiology

Anaphylaxis is a systemic, potentially fatal hypersensitivity reaction, usually IgE-mediated (food, venom, drugs) but sometimes via direct mast cell activation. Cross-linking of IgE on mast cells and basophils triggers body-wide degranulation releasing histamine, tryptase, PAF, leukotrienes, prostaglandin D2 and bradykinin.

Cardiovascular: widespread vasodilation (partly NO-mediated) and a sharp rise in capillary permeability push a large fraction of plasma volume into the interstitium within minutes, producing a combination of distributive and hypovolaemic shock with reduced venous return; PAF and mediators depress myocardial function. Respiratory: bronchial smooth muscle constriction, mucosal oedema, mucus secretion and upper airway/laryngeal angioedema cause wheeze, stridor and hypoxia. Skin/GI: urticaria, flushing, cramping, vomiting, diarrhoea.

Death most often results from airway obstruction or refractory shock. Features can be biphasic (recurrence hours later) and patients with poorly controlled asthma, on beta-blockers, or with delayed adrenaline are at higher risk. Standing or walking suddenly can precipitate collapse (empty ventricle), so patients are kept supine.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Allergen exposure: IgE cross-linking on mast cells / basophils
  2. Mass release of histamine, tryptase, PAF, leukotrienes, PGD2
  3. Vasodilation and capillary leak: distributive plus hypovolaemic shock
  4. Bronchospasm and upper airway oedema: hypoxia, stridor
  5. Myocardial depression: refractory shock, cardiac arrest

Asthma (Severe Exacerbation)

Pathophysiology

Asthma is chronic Th2-driven airway inflammation (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, eosinophils, mast cells) with airway hyper-responsiveness. During an exacerbation, an allergen, virus or irritant triggers three overlapping processes: (1) bronchospasm, acute contraction of airway smooth muscle driven by vagal cholinergic tone and mediators (histamine, leukotriene D4); (2) mucosal inflammation and oedema; and (3) mucus hypersecretion and plugging.

Narrowing increases expiratory resistance, so air is trapped (dynamic hyperinflation, auto-PEEP). Hyperinflation flattens the diaphragm, increases work of breathing and raises intrathoracic pressure, which can impair venous return (pulsus paradoxus, hypotension). V/Q mismatch causes hypoxaemia. Early on the patient hyperventilates (low PaCO2); a 'normal' or rising PaCO2 signals exhaustion. A silent chest means airflow is too low to produce wheeze and is an ominous sign.

A late-phase inflammatory response 4-8 hours after the trigger can cause relapse, which is why corticosteroids are given even though they act slowly.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Trigger: allergen, virus, irritant, exercise
  2. Smooth muscle contraction, mucosal oedema, mucus plugging
  3. Expiratory flow limitation, air trapping, hyperinflation
  4. Increased work of breathing, V/Q mismatch, hypoxia
  5. Fatigue, hypercapnia, silent chest, respiratory arrest

Autonomic Dysreflexia

Pathophysiology

Autonomic dysreflexia occurs in patients with spinal cord injury at or above T6. Sympathetic preganglionic neurons for the splanchnic bed sit at T5-L2 and normally receive descending inhibitory control from the brainstem. After a high lesion that control is lost, so below the lesion the sympathetic outflow is isolated and hyper-reactive.

A noxious stimulus below the injury (most often a full or blocked bladder, then bowel impaction, pressure injury, ingrown toenail, tight clothing, fracture, labour) sends afferent signals into the cord that trigger an uncontrolled, mass sympathetic reflex. The splanchnic vascular bed and limb vessels vasoconstrict, producing sudden severe hypertension (typically 20-40 mmHg or more above the patient's baseline, which is often low, around 90-110 systolic).

Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch detect the hypertension and drive vagal slowing (reflex bradycardia) and vasodilation above the lesion (flushing, sweating, headache, nasal congestion), but the descending inhibitory signal cannot cross the lesion to relax the vessels below. Untreated, the surge can cause intracranial haemorrhage, seizures, retinal haemorrhage, pulmonary oedema or death.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Noxious stimulus below lesion (bladder, bowel, skin)
  2. Afferent signal enters cord and triggers isolated sympathetic discharge
  3. Splanchnic and peripheral vasoconstriction: severe hypertension
  4. Baroreflex: bradycardia, flushing and sweating above the lesion
  5. Inhibitory control blocked at lesion: hypertension persists, risk of stroke

Behavioural Disturbance (Acute)

Pathophysiology

Acute behavioural disturbance is a presentation, not a diagnosis: severe agitation, aggression or psychosis that poses immediate risk to the patient or others. Causes are psychiatric (mania, schizophrenia), toxic (stimulants, alcohol, hallucinogens, anticholinergics), withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), medical (hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, sepsis, head injury, delirium, metabolic or endocrine disease) and dementia-related.

Neurochemically, agitation reflects excess dopaminergic and noradrenergic drive (mesolimbic dopamine hyperactivity in psychosis; amphetamine and cocaine blocking monoamine reuptake) with failure of GABAergic inhibition (alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal) and, in delirium, a cholinergic deficit with dopamine excess. Features that suggest a medical cause include age over 45 with no psychiatric history, abnormal vital signs, disorientation and fluctuating consciousness.

The danger is physiological as well as behavioural: prolonged exertion and struggling cause hyperthermia, lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemia, while prone restraint risks positional asphyxia. This 'agitation-hyperthermia-acidosis spiral' can precede sudden cardiac arrest. Always check blood glucose and treat reversible causes.

  1. Precipitant: psychosis, intoxication, withdrawal, delirium, pain, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia
  2. Excess dopamine/noradrenaline drive, reduced GABA inhibition
  3. Agitation, aggression, struggling: hyperthermia and lactic acidosis
  4. Rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, exhaustion

Bradycardia (Symptomatic)

Pathophysiology

Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. The sinoatrial node (normally 60-100/min) and AV node are slowed by the vagus nerve: acetylcholine acts on M2 muscarinic receptors, increasing K+ efflux (IK,ACh) and reducing If and Ca2+ currents. Sympathetic input (beta-1) does the opposite. Bradycardia results from sinus node dysfunction, excessive vagal tone (inferior MI via Bezold-Jarisch reflex, vomiting, pain, suction), AV conduction block, drugs (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin), hypoxia, hypothermia, hyperkalaemia and raised intracranial pressure (Cushing's reflex).

When rate falls and stroke volume cannot compensate, cardiac output and perfusion fall, producing hypotension, syncope, chest pain, altered consciousness, heart failure or shock. Where the block is matters: nodal (supra-Hisian) sites respond to atropine because vagal tone is a major contributor, whereas infranodal block (Mobitz II, complete block with wide QRS) is not vagally mediated, produces unreliable ventricular escape rhythms, and may be worsened by atropine.

Treat the cause (hypoxia, drugs, hyperkalaemia) in parallel; if drugs fail the next steps are adrenaline and transcutaneous pacing.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Reduced SA node firing or AV conduction (vagal, ischaemic, drug, degenerative)
  2. Heart rate falls: cardiac output = HR x SV decreases
  3. Hypotension, hypoperfusion: syncope, chest pain, confusion
  4. Infranodal block: slow unstable escape rhythm, risk of asystole

Cardiogenic Pulmonary Oedema (CPO)

Pathophysiology

Fluid movement across the pulmonary capillary follows Starling forces. Normally capillary hydrostatic pressure is low (about 8-12 mmHg) and lymphatics clear any leak. In acute left heart failure (ischaemia, hypertensive crisis, valve disease, arrhythmia, fluid overload) the left ventricle cannot eject, so end-diastolic and left atrial pressure rise and transmit back to the pulmonary veins. When capillary pressure exceeds roughly 20-25 mmHg, transudate floods the interstitium then the alveoli, stiffening the lung, increasing work of breathing and causing a shunt (blood perfusing fluid-filled alveoli) with hypoxaemia.

A vicious cycle follows: hypoxaemia and distress cause a sympathetic surge, raising systemic vascular resistance (afterload) and heart rate; the RAAS retains salt and water; worsening ventricular performance further raises filling pressures. In hypertensive 'flash' pulmonary oedema the problem is often acute volume redistribution from the venous reservoir to the lungs (afterload mismatch) rather than total body fluid overload, which is why vasodilators work so well.

Patients are anxious, sweaty, tachycardic and orthopnoeic with crackles and pink frothy sputum in severe cases.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. LV failure: end-diastolic and left atrial pressure rise
  2. Pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure exceeds about 25 mmHg
  3. Fluid leaks into interstitium and alveoli: stiff lung, shunt, hypoxia
  4. Sympathetic and RAAS activation raise afterload and fluid retention
  5. Worsening failure and respiratory exhaustion

COPD Exacerbation

Pathophysiology

COPD results from chronic exposure (mainly cigarette smoke) causing neutrophil/macrophage inflammation, oxidant stress and a protease-antiprotease imbalance. This produces chronic bronchitis (mucus gland and goblet cell hyperplasia, impaired ciliary clearance, airway wall fibrosis) and emphysema (elastin destruction in the alveolar walls with loss of elastic recoil and airway tethering, so small airways collapse on expiration). Airflow limitation is largely fixed, but a reversible component remains, notably increased cholinergic tone.

An exacerbation, usually triggered by infection or pollutants, adds acute inflammation, bronchospasm, oedema and mucus. Expiratory flow limitation worsens, causing dynamic hyperinflation and auto-PEEP, flattening the diaphragm and sharply increasing work of breathing. Ventilation-perfusion mismatch causes hypoxaemia and, as the respiratory muscles tire, CO2 retention. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction raises right heart strain (cor pulmonale).

Oxygen must be titrated: excess oxygen worsens V/Q matching and reduces the Haldane effect, raising PaCO2, so a target SpO2 of 88-92% is used.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Infection or irritant on chronic airway disease
  2. Bronchospasm, oedema and mucus: worse expiratory flow limitation
  3. Dynamic hyperinflation, auto-PEEP, high work of breathing
  4. V/Q mismatch: hypoxaemia; tiring muscles: hypercapnia
  5. Respiratory failure, right heart strain

CPR-Induced Consciousness (CPRIC)

Pathophysiology

During cardiac arrest, blood flow to the brain depends on the perfusion pressure generated by compressions (the 'cardiac pump' and 'thoracic pump' mechanisms, plus the coronary perfusion pressure that supports the heart). High-quality, early CPR, particularly in patients with a short arrest time and intact brain, can produce cerebral perfusion pressure above the threshold for consciousness, so the patient shows eye-opening, purposeful movement, groaning, resisting airway devices or speaking.

This is physiologically good news (a sign of effective CPR and neurological reserve) but creates two problems: the patient may interfere with CPR, airway management or defibrillation, reducing the quality of resuscitation; and they are likely to experience pain from compressions (rib/sternal fractures) and distress, with possible awareness. Withholding sedation is ethically uncomfortable; the aim is to treat distress and interference with minimal impact on perfusion.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. High-quality CPR generates cerebral perfusion pressure above threshold
  2. Consciousness returns: movement, eye-opening, groaning
  3. Interference with compressions and airway: poorer CPR quality
  4. Pain and distress from compressions, possible awareness

Hyperkalaemia

Pathophysiology

The resting membrane potential (about -90 mV in ventricular myocytes) is set mainly by the K+ gradient (Nernst/GHK). Raising extracellular K+ makes the membrane less negative (partial depolarisation). Initially this brings the cell closer to threshold (increased excitability), but with sustained depolarisation the fast Na+ channels are inactivated (voltage-dependent), so cells become less excitable and conduction slows. Repolarisation accelerates because K+ conductance increases.

This explains the ECG progression: peaked T waves (rapid repolarisation), then flattened P waves and prolonged PR, widened QRS, and finally a sine-wave pattern leading to VF or asystole. Skeletal muscle weakness and paraesthesia occur. Causes include reduced renal excretion (AKI, CKD, spironolactone, ACE inhibitors), transcellular shifts (acidosis, insulin deficiency in DKA, rhabdomyolysis and crush reperfusion, burns, tissue necrosis), adrenal insufficiency and high intake. Rapid rises are far more dangerous than chronic elevations.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Raised extracellular K+: membrane partially depolarised
  2. Fast Na+ channels inactivate: slow conduction, reduced excitability
  3. Peaked T, flat P, wide QRS, sine wave
  4. Bradycardia, heart block, VF or asystole

Hypoglycaemia

Pathophysiology

The brain uses about 120 g of glucose a day with almost no energy stores, entering neurons through insulin-independent GLUT1/GLUT3 transporters. When blood glucose falls below about 4 mmol/L, a hierarchy of counter-regulatory responses is triggered: insulin secretion falls, then glucagon and adrenaline are released (stimulating hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis), followed by cortisol and growth hormone.

Adrenaline release produces the autonomic symptoms (sweating, tremor, tachycardia, anxiety, hunger). If glucose keeps falling, neuroglycopenia develops (confusion, irritability, slurred speech, seizures, coma) because neuronal ATP production fails. Prolonged severe hypoglycaemia causes neuronal death and cardiac arrhythmia (QT prolongation).

Common causes: insulin or sulfonylurea excess or mistimed dosing, missed meals, exercise, alcohol (blocks gluconeogenesis), liver disease, sepsis, adrenal insufficiency. Patients with long-standing diabetes or taking beta-blockers may lack warning symptoms.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Glucose falls (insulin or sulfonylurea excess, fasting, alcohol, illness)
  2. Counter-regulation: glucagon and adrenaline, then cortisol; autonomic symptoms
  3. Neuroglycopenia: confusion, seizures, coma
  4. Neuronal injury and arrhythmia if prolonged

Meningococcal Disease

Pathophysiology

Neisseria meningitidis is a Gram-negative diplococcus that colonises the nasopharynx. In susceptible people it crosses the mucosa, evades complement with its polysaccharide capsule and enters the bloodstream, where it can seed the meninges (meningitis) or cause overwhelming septicaemia, or both.

The key virulence factor is lipo-oligosaccharide endotoxin, which binds TLR4/MD-2 on monocytes and endothelium, activating NF-kappa-B and producing a massive release of TNF-alpha, IL-1 and IL-6. Consequences: widespread vasodilation and capillary leak (distributive and hypovolaemic shock), myocardial depression, endothelial injury with tissue factor expression, consumption of protein C, protein S and antithrombin, and disseminated intravascular coagulation with microvascular thrombosis. This produces the non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash (purpura fulminans), limb ischaemia and adrenal haemorrhage.

In meningitis, bacteria cross the blood-brain barrier and the inflammatory response in the subarachnoid space raises intracranial pressure through cerebral oedema and impaired CSF absorption, causing headache, neck stiffness, photophobia and reduced consciousness. Deterioration can be extremely fast (hours), so antibiotics must not be delayed for diagnostic certainty.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Nasopharyngeal colonisation: invasion of bloodstream
  2. Endotoxin (LOS) activates TLR4: TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6 surge
  3. Vasodilation, capillary leak, myocardial depression: septic shock
  4. Tissue factor activation, loss of natural anticoagulants: DIC, purpura fulminans
  5. Meningeal invasion: raised ICP, reduced GCS

Nausea and Vomiting

Pathophysiology

Vomiting is a coordinated protective reflex organised by the emetic centre (nucleus tractus solitarius and adjacent reticular formation) in the medulla. It integrates input from: (1) the chemoreceptor trigger zone in the area postrema (no effective blood-brain barrier), rich in dopamine D2, serotonin 5-HT3, NK1 and opioid receptors, which detects toxins, drugs and metabolic disturbances in blood; (2) vagal and spinal afferents from the gut, where enterochromaffin cells release serotonin onto 5-HT3 receptors on vagal terminals in response to irritation, distension or toxins; (3) the vestibular system (histamine H1 and muscarinic receptors); and (4) the cerebral cortex and limbic system (anxiety, pain, smells).

Efferent output coordinates a deep inspiration, glottic closure, abdominal wall and diaphragm contraction with relaxation of the gastro-oesophageal sphincter, so gastric contents are expelled. Consequences include dehydration, hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis, aspiration, and rises in intra-abdominal, intracranial and intra-ocular pressure (important in head injury and penetrating eye injury).

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Trigger: toxin/drug/pain at CTZ, gut irritation, motion, anxiety
  2. Serotonin and dopamine signalling to emetic centre via 5-HT3 and D2 receptors
  3. Coordinated reflex: retching, vomiting
  4. Dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, aspiration, raised ICP/IOP

Nerve Agent Poisoning

Pathophysiology

Nerve agents (sarin, soman, tabun, VX) are organophosphorus compounds that phosphorylate the serine residue in the active site of acetylcholinesterase (AChE), preventing breakdown of acetylcholine (ACh). The bond may become permanent through 'aging' (dealkylation; soman ages in minutes), after which the enzyme cannot be reactivated by an oxime.

Accumulated ACh overstimulates three receptor groups: muscarinic (parasympathetic organs and sweat glands: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI cramps, emesis, miosis, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, bradycardia), nicotinic (neuromuscular junction: fasciculations, weakness then paralysis; autonomic ganglia: tachycardia and hypertension), and central receptors (anxiety, seizures, coma, central respiratory depression). Death is usually from respiratory failure caused by bronchorrhoea and bronchospasm, paralysis of the diaphragm and intercostals and loss of central drive. Seizures add neuronal injury.

Vapour exposure acts within seconds to minutes; liquid on skin may delay symptoms. Decontamination and PPE protect the rescuer and are a priority.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Nerve agent phosphorylates AChE (aging makes this permanent)
  2. ACh accumulates at muscarinic, nicotinic and central synapses
  3. Cholinergic crisis: secretions, bronchospasm, bradycardia, miosis, fasciculations
  4. Seizures, coma, diaphragmatic paralysis
  5. Respiratory failure and death

Organophosphate Poisoning

Pathophysiology

Organophosphate (OP) pesticides (e.g. chlorpyrifos, malathion, fenthion) act exactly like nerve agents: they phosphorylate acetylcholinesterase, so ACh accumulates at muscarinic, nicotinic and central synapses and causes a cholinergic crisis. Exposure is via ingestion, skin or inhalation; many OPs are lipid-soluble, so absorption and effects can be delayed and prolonged, and the solvent (often a hydrocarbon) can add aspiration or CNS toxicity.

Muscarinic effects (DUMBELS/SLUDGE: diarrhoea, urination, miosis, bronchorrhoea/bronchospasm, emesis, lacrimation, salivation, bradycardia) dominate early; nicotinic effects (fasciculations, weakness) and central effects (agitation, seizures, coma) follow. Respiratory failure arises from bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, respiratory muscle weakness and central depression. Later problems include the intermediate syndrome (proximal and respiratory muscle weakness 24-96 h after exposure) and delayed neuropathy.

Carbamate insecticides also inhibit AChE, but reversibly, so effects are shorter-lived.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. OP ingestion/absorption inhibits AChE
  2. Cholinergic crisis: bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, bradycardia, miosis, vomiting
  3. Fasciculations and weakness; seizures and coma
  4. Respiratory failure; later intermediate syndrome

Overdose (Opioid)

Pathophysiology

Opioids (heroin, fentanyl, oxycodone, methadone, morphine) act on mu-opioid receptors. In the brainstem (pre-Bötzinger complex, parabrachial/Kölliker-Fuse nuclei) this reduces the sensitivity of the respiratory centre to CO2 and slows the respiratory rhythm generator, so respiratory rate falls first, then tidal volume, then apnoea. Loss of upper airway tone adds obstruction, and CNS depression reduces airway protection.

The resulting hypoxaemia and hypercapnia cause bradycardia, hypotension, acidosis and eventually cardiac arrest. Mu-receptor stimulation in the Edinger-Westphal nucleus produces pinpoint pupils; gut effects include reduced motility. Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema and, with rapid fentanyl exposure, chest wall rigidity can occur. Long-acting opioids (methadone) and extended exposures can outlast naloxone's duration (30-80 min), so re-sedation is possible.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Opioid binds mu receptors in brainstem respiratory centres
  2. Blunted CO2 response; slow, shallow breathing, airway obstruction
  3. Hypoxaemia and hypercapnia, coma, pinpoint pupils
  4. Bradycardia, hypotension, cardiac arrest

Penetrating Eye Injury

Pathophysiology

The globe is a pressurised, relatively rigid sphere (normal intra-ocular pressure about 10-21 mmHg) maintained by the corneoscleral wall. A full-thickness laceration or penetrating object breaches this barrier. Any rise in intra-ocular pressure, from vomiting, coughing, straining, crying or external pressure, can expel uveal tissue, vitreous, lens or retina through the wound, causing permanent visual loss. Penetration also allows microbial entry (endophthalmitis) and may involve intra-ocular foreign bodies.

Vomiting causes a Valsalva-type rise in venous and intra-ocular pressure, so anti-emesis is a vision-saving intervention. Care includes protecting (not patching or pressing on) the eye with a rigid shield, leaving embedded objects in place, minimising movement, and treating pain (opioids can cause nausea).

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Full-thickness breach of cornea or sclera
  2. Raised IOP (vomiting, straining, pressure) pushes contents out of the wound
  3. Extrusion of vitreous, uvea, lens; risk of infection
  4. Permanent visual loss

Post ROSC Sedation

Pathophysiology

After return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) the patient has post-cardiac arrest syndrome: global ischaemia-reperfusion injury. During arrest ATP depletes, ion gradients fail and Ca2+ overloads cells; reperfusion then produces a burst of reactive oxygen species, mitochondrial dysfunction, glutamate excitotoxicity and inflammation. Cerebral autoregulation is impaired, so the injured brain is vulnerable to both hypotension (ischaemia) and hypertension, hypercapnia or hyperoxia (oedema, further injury). Myocardial stunning and vasoplegia cause haemodynamic instability.

Patients who regain some consciousness become agitated, resist the endotracheal tube or ventilator (patient-ventilator dyssynchrony), cough, shiver and surge sympathetically. This increases cerebral and total metabolic oxygen demand (CMRO2), intracranial pressure, CO2 production and blood pressure swings. Sedation and analgesia aim to prevent these, facilitate controlled ventilation and transport, and allow temperature management, while avoiding hypotension that would worsen cerebral perfusion.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Ischaemia-reperfusion injury: ROS, Ca2+ overload, excitotoxicity
  2. Impaired autoregulation and myocardial dysfunction
  3. Agitation, ventilator dyssynchrony, sympathetic surge: higher CMRO2, ICP
  4. Secondary brain injury; haemodynamic instability

Post Intraosseous Access

Pathophysiology

The intraosseous (IO) needle sits in the medullary cavity, a rigid, non-compressible space within bone that contains a rich venous sinusoid network and is innervated by nociceptors in the endosteum and periosteum, many of which are pressure-sensitive. Insertion is usually less painful than infusion. When fluid or drugs are pushed in a conscious patient, the intramedullary pressure rises abruptly, stimulating these mechanosensitive afferents (A-delta and C fibres) and producing severe, often unexpectedly intense pain that persists until the cavity pressure equilibrates.

Local anaesthetic before the flush blocks the pain pathway at the source, so the pressure rise is no longer perceived. This matters because uncontrolled pain causes sympathetic activation, patient movement (dislodging the device) and distress.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Fluid or drug flushed into the medullary cavity
  2. Intramedullary pressure rises in a rigid space
  3. Pressure-sensitive endosteal/periosteal nociceptors fire
  4. Severe pain, sympathetic surge, movement

Post Partum Care (Haemorrhage)

Pathophysiology

At term, uterine blood flow is roughly 500-700 mL/min, delivered through spiral arteries to the placental bed. After the placenta separates, bleeding is controlled mainly mechanically: the interlaced myometrial fibres contract (the 'living ligatures') and compress the spiral arteries, with clotting a secondary mechanism. PPH is blood loss of 500 mL or more after vaginal birth (1000 mL after caesarean) or any loss producing haemodynamic compromise.

The causes are the four Ts: Tone (uterine atony, about 70-80% of cases), Trauma (tears, uterine rupture or inversion), Tissue (retained placenta or products) and Thrombin (coagulopathy). Atony is more likely with an over-distended uterus (twins, polyhydramnios), prolonged or augmented labour, high parity, infection and some anaesthetic agents.

Pregnant women have a 40-50% increase in blood volume, so they can compensate for large losses and then deteriorate suddenly (hypotension is a late sign). Loss of oxygen-carrying capacity, then DIC from dilutional and consumptive coagulopathy, can follow.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Third stage: placenta separates; uterine blood flow of about 500-700 mL/min exposed
  2. Uterus fails to contract (atony) or other cause (trauma, tissue, thrombin)
  3. Spiral arteries stay open: rapid blood loss
  4. Late shock (maternal volume expansion masks early signs); DIC

Prehospital Thrombolysis (for STEMI)

Pathophysiology

In STEMI an occlusive coronary thrombus, containing platelets and fibrin, completely stops antegrade flow. Myocardial necrosis proceeds as a wavefront from endocardium to epicardium, making reperfusion time-critical ('time is muscle'). Primary PCI is the preferred reperfusion strategy; where PCI cannot be reached within the target time, fibrinolysis is the alternative. NSW Ambulance's regional program uses ECG transmission, a reading service and consent before treatment.

Fibrinolysis exploits the natural clot-lysing system: plasminogen is converted to plasmin, which digests fibrin. Dissolving the clot, however, exposes clot-bound thrombin, which is itself pro-thrombotic and activates platelets, so antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy are needed to prevent re-occlusion. Reperfusion often causes transient arrhythmias (accelerated idioventricular rhythm, bradycardia) and about 30% of patients fail to reperfuse and need urgent rescue PCI.

Bleeding, especially intracranial, is the main risk, which is why eligibility criteria are strict (NSW Ambulance uses conservative criteria such as heart rate under 50, chest pain over 6 hours, anticoagulant use, recent stroke and very high BP).

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Occlusive platelet-fibrin thrombus: no antegrade flow
  2. Transmural ischaemia, necrosis spreads over hours
  3. Fibrinolysis dissolves clot, but exposes thrombin: re-occlusion risk
  4. Dual antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy hold the open vessel
  5. Bleeding risk, reperfusion arrhythmias; failed lysis needs rescue PCI

Seizures (Status Epilepticus)

Pathophysiology

A seizure is a transient episode of abnormal, excessive and hypersynchronous neuronal discharge. It reflects an imbalance between excitation (glutamate via AMPA/NMDA receptors) and inhibition (GABA via GABA-A receptors). Provoking factors include epilepsy (missed medication), hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, infection, head injury, stroke, toxins and drug withdrawal.

Most seizures stop spontaneously within a minute or two. In status epilepticus (continuous seizure for 5 minutes or longer, or repeated seizures without recovery) the self-terminating mechanisms fail. With time, GABA-A receptors are internalised from the synapse and NMDA receptors increase, making the seizure progressively resistant to benzodiazepines; early treatment is therefore much more effective.

Systemic consequences escalate over time: hypoxia from airway compromise, lactic and respiratory acidosis, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, hyperglycaemia then hypoglycaemia, raised intracranial pressure and, beyond about 30 minutes, neuronal death. Aspiration and injury are major risks.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Excess glutamate excitation or loss of GABA inhibition
  2. Hypersynchronous neuronal firing; seizure fails to self-terminate (status)
  3. GABA-A internalisation, NMDA upregulation: drug resistance
  4. Hypoxia, acidosis, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis
  5. Neuronal injury after about 30 min

Tachycardia (Refractory VT/VF)

Pathophysiology

Ventricular arrhythmias arise by three mechanisms: re-entry (a circuit around scar or ischaemic tissue with unidirectional block and slow conduction), enhanced automaticity and triggered activity (early or delayed afterdepolarisations). Monomorphic VT usually reflects a fixed scar circuit; VF is a chaotic state of multiple wandering wavelets with no organised contraction, so there is no cardiac output.

VF/pulseless VT is termed refractory when it persists or recurs after repeated defibrillation. Common drivers are ongoing myocardial ischaemia or infarction, catecholamine excess (including repeated adrenaline doses), acidosis, electrolyte disturbance (hypo/hyperkalaemia, hypomagnesaemia), hypothermia, toxins and structural heart disease. Each failed shock and prolonged arrest reduces the likelihood of success as myocardial energy stores fall.

Stable VT with a pulse and adverse features is treated with synchronised cardioversion; amiodarone is used for refractory or recurrent episodes.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. Substrate (ischaemia, scar, electrolyte or autonomic disturbance)
  2. Re-entry or triggered activity produces VT; degeneration to VF
  3. No cardiac output; refractory if shocks repeatedly fail
  4. Acidosis, catecholamine surge and energy depletion perpetuate the arrhythmia

Torsades De Pointes (TdP)

Pathophysiology

Torsades de pointes is polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with a characteristic twisting QRS axis, occurring in the setting of QT prolongation (delayed ventricular repolarisation). Causes: congenital long QT syndromes, QT-prolonging drugs (class Ia/III antiarrhythmics, macrolides, antipsychotics including droperidol, ondansetron, methadone), hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia and bradycardia.

Prolonged repolarisation (reduced IKr, so the action potential plateau lasts longer) allows L-type Ca2+ channels to reactivate during phase 2/3, producing early afterdepolarisations (EADs). When an EAD reaches threshold during the vulnerable period it triggers a premature beat, typically on a pause (long-short-long sequence), which can start polymorphic VT. TdP may be self-limiting (palpitations, syncope) or degenerate to VF.

Pathophysiological cascade

  1. QT prolongation (drug, electrolyte, congenital, bradycardia)
  2. Delayed repolarisation: L-type Ca2+ reactivation causes early afterdepolarisations
  3. Premature beat on T wave (often after a pause)
  4. Polymorphic VT (torsades); may degenerate to VF

Contraindications, Precautions and Key Notes

  • Stop QT-prolonging drugs, correct potassium and magnesium, and defibrillate if pulseless or unstable (synchronised cardioversion if conscious with a pulse). Pacing may be needed for TdP due to bradycardia or heart block.
  • ANZCOR advises avoiding amiodarone in TdP because it prolongs the QT interval. Dose and availability of magnesium for paramedics should be confirmed in the NSWA CPG.

Fluids (Isotonic Crystalloid)

General Mechanism and Outcome

Mechanism of Action (MOA): Isotonic crystalloids (0.9% sodium chloride, Compound Sodium Lactate/Hartmann's) have a tonicity similar to plasma. They distribute through the extracellular fluid space (about one-third of total body water); roughly 25% of the infused volume stays in the intravascular space for a limited time, so large volumes are needed (about 3-4 mL per 1 mL of blood lost). Intravascular volume expansion raises venous return and preload.

Therapeutic Outcome: The goal is to raise preload, stroke volume and cardiac output (Frank-Starling), restoring blood pressure and organ perfusion. Limits: dilution of clotting factors, hypothermia from cold fluid, and hyperchloraemic acidosis with large volumes of 0.9% saline. Compound Sodium Lactate (lactate is metabolised to bicarbonate in the liver) is less likely to cause hyperchloraemia but large volumes can still dilute coagulation factors and worsen acidosis (the 'lethal triad' of hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy).
