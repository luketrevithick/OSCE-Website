Definitions & Core Concepts

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Mass Casualty Incident (MCI) WHO definition: An incident which generates more patients at one time than locally available resources can manage using routine procedures. It requires exceptional emergency arrangements and additional or extraordinary assistance. In practical terms — the number of patients exceeds the resources available on scene.
Major Incident An incident involving, or with the potential to involve, a large number of casualties which can be adequately managed by available resources, but requires a significant and coordinated response.
Disaster An event impacting a community which causes serious disruption to the functions of that community and generates a large number of casualties, overwhelming the capacity of immediately available resources to manage the situation.
Combat Agency The lead operational body designated by law or plan to manage a particular type of emergency or major incident (e.g. Police for terrorism, Fire & Rescue for HAZMAT).

Distinguishing the Terms — The Key Question

The distinguishing factor between these terms is not the number of casualties alone, but the relationship between demand and available resources:

ScenarioCan resources cope?Classification
Multi-vehicle crash, 8 patients, adequate crews and hospitals nearbyYes, with coordinationMajor Incident
Bus rollover, 40 patients, resources on scene overwhelmedNo — exceeds routine capacityMass Casualty Incident
Earthquake destroying community infrastructure and hospitalsNo — capacity itself is destroyedDisaster

A Mass Casualty Incident can escalate into a Disaster if the surrounding health and emergency infrastructure is itself compromised.

Overview & Historical Context

The Beginnings of Triage

Triage is the process of sorting casualties for treatment priority. Its origins are military:

  • Formalised by the Surgeon General to Napoleon's Army of the Rhine, who prioritised treatment by clinical urgency rather than rank.
  • Refined through the Korean War, which introduced a four-tiered triage system — the conceptual ancestor of the modern Red/Yellow/Green/Black categories.

These incidents drove significant reform of Australian mass casualty doctrine, command structures, and inter-agency planning arrangements.

Types of Major / Mass Casualty Incident

MCIs are not limited to transport crashes. Recognised categories include:

• Biosecurity• Criminal• Cyclone • Earthquake• Environmental• Fire — Bushfire • Fire — Urban• Flood• Food safety • Hazardous substances• Industrial• Infrastructure failure • Landslide• Maritime / Coastal• Storm • Terrorism• Transport• Tsunami

Why Mass Casualty Incidents Are Challenging

  • Dynamic: the situation, hazards, and casualty numbers evolve continuously.
  • Demanding: physically and cognitively taxing, often over extended periods.
  • Confronting: high casualty numbers, distressing injuries, and mortality.
  • Chaotic: the "fog" of an incident scene resists normal routine practice.
  • Communications: consistently cited as the most significant challenge to achieving optimal outcomes — spanning interpersonal communication, platforms of communication (radio, phone, face-to-face), and the forms and contents of communication (structured reporting).
  • Multiple agencies: coordinating Police, Fire & Rescue, SES, Ambulance, and others under one command philosophy.
  • Forensic crime scene: when the incident is also a crime scene (e.g. terrorism), evidence preservation constrains movement and intervention.

Ambulance Service Operational Principles

General Priorities

  1. Do not become a casualty. Your safety, then your crew's, precedes patient care.
  2. Report accurately. Everything downstream depends on the quality of your early information.
  3. Do the greatest good for the greatest number. Resource-limited decision-making, not individual-patient medicine.
  4. Bring order to chaos. Structure, command, and systematic process are the antidote to scene chaos.

Key Groups Present at an MCI Scene

  • Patients with physical injuries
  • People with little or no physical injury, but psychologically affected
  • People who initially leave the scene and later self-present to a health facility
  • Spontaneous (untrained, unaffiliated) volunteers

Safety

  • Continuous assessment of risks and hazards
  • Personal Protective Equipment appropriate to the zone and hazard
  • Other safety strategies: scene lighting, traffic management, structural stability checks, situational awareness

Communication

Communication is the single most cited barrier to successful MCI management. Principles include:

  • Use of dedicated radio channels for the incident
  • Efficient and effective radio use — concise and pertinent messaging
  • Attention to interpersonal communication, not just technology
  • Avoidance of alternative/unofficial communication methods (e.g. personal mobile phones) which fragment the common operating picture

Navigation & Vehicles

Navigation: latitude and longitude, alternative location methods (e.g. what3words), and standard map reading remain essential when street addressing fails (e.g. rural, off-road, or waterway incidents).

Vehicles:

  • Emergency beacons activated for the first arriving vehicle only (to preserve scene approach for later vehicles and avoid confusing the overall picture)
  • Keys remain with the vehicle
  • Access and egress routes must be maintained at all times
  • Vehicles arriving after the initial response report to the Staging Officer or Forward Commander and remain at the Staging Area until directed to the incident site or loading point

Other Operational Principles

  • Guide dogs and support dogs require specific management consideration
  • Documentation: contemporaneous notes, use of a scribe at command posts, and Control Centre logging of all major decisions and timelines

The Operational Response — Four Stages

Stage 1: Recon and Report (First Ambulance on Scene)

The Windscreen Report

On arrival, the first crew conducts a rapid 360° visual assessment from the safety of the vehicle — without committing to the scene or becoming a casualty themselves. This "windscreen survey" forms the basis of the very first, brief situation report passed to Control. It exists to trigger an early warning to the system (additional resourcing, other agency notification) before the fuller, more detailed METHANE report can be compiled.

Immediate actions:

  • Designate an interim Forward Commander (Silver Command)
  • Transmit the windscreen report
  • Compile and transmit a full METHANE report

Stage 2: Triage SIEVE

  • Perform primary Triage SIEVE across all casualties
  • Deliver immediate life-saving interventions only (see dedicated section below)
  • Move walking, ambulant patients away from danger toward the Survivor Reception Area

Stage 3: Activate Posts, Supervisors, and Channel

A dedicated incident radio channel is designated. The Ambulance Commander attends the Emergency Operations Centre (EOC) as Bronze Command, while the on-scene Forward Commander operates as Silver Command. Six functional posts are then established (detailed in full in the Scene Stations section below):

  1. Forward Command Post (FCP)
  2. Staging Area (SA)
  3. Incident Site (IS)
  4. Casualty Clearing Station (CCS)
  5. Loading Point (LP)
  6. Survivor Reception Area

Stage 4: Hot Debrief and Equipment Rehabilitation

Crews complete a hot (immediate, on-scene) debrief and equipment/vehicle rehabilitation before being released from the incident. Release of crews can only occur after this phase — even where an individual crew feels their task is complete, structured stand-down protects both welfare and equipment/documentation integrity.

Triage: SIEVE and SORT

Triage Fundamentals

Definition: The sorting of patients according to the urgency of their need for care.

Objective: Achieve the greatest good for the greatest number by rapidly determining clinical and transport priorities, and prioritising treatment and transport based on likelihood of survival.

Indication: Whenever the number of patients exceeds the number of skilled personnel on scene.

System used: SMART Triage, approved by the (then) Civil Aviation Authority-linked ambulance standards body in 2010, comprising two sequential tools — Triage SIEVE (primary, at the Incident Site) and Triage SORT (secondary, at the Casualty Clearing Station).

SMART Tag Kit — Equipment

  • 20 interchangeable SMART triage tags
  • 10 Deceased SMART triage tags
  • 20 Contamination / CBRN tags
  • 1 paediatric SMART tape
  • 2 pencils
  • 5 light sticks (low-light patient identification)
  • 1 Triage SIEVE / casualty count reference card
  • Barcode on the tag back for Loading Point tracking

Each tag records vital information about treatments given, has a glowing light stick attachment for visibility in all conditions, clearly highlights infected/contaminated casualties, stays attached to the casualty throughout decontamination, and carries a detachable barcoded transport strip for enhanced tracking.

Method 1 — Triage SIEVE (Initial / Primary Triage)

Description: Performed in the field, prior to moving patients. Objective: Determine urgency of intervention rapidly. Two versions exist — a flowchart card for adults, and a flowchart printed on tape for paediatrics (Paediatric SMART Tape).

Important Considerations

  • Triage often requires reversing normal clinical priorities — this can be very challenging psychologically for clinicians trained to treat the sickest patient first.
  • Triage is a dynamic, continuous process — categories change as patients deteriorate, improve, or as further information becomes available. Re-triage at every stage of movement.
  • Triage can be performed by any competent paramedic — there is no requirement for the highest clinical level on scene to perform triage.

Immediate Life-Saving Interventions (ILS) During Triage SIEVE

The Resource-Limited Mindset

At the Incident Site, the objective is rapid movement through every patient — "tag and move" — rather than definitive treatment of any single patient. Every minute spent on one patient is a minute not spent identifying and tagging others who may also be salvageable. Interventions during the SIEVE phase are therefore deliberately minimal, and confined to interventions that can be performed in seconds and that address an immediately reversible threat to life.

What TO Do During Triage SIEVE

  • Control catastrophic external haemorrhage — tourniquet application or wound packing/haemostatic dressing; this is the single highest-yield intervention at this stage.
  • Open and clear the airway — simple manoeuvres only (head tilt/chin lift or jaw thrust, finger sweep of visible obstruction, positioning), used specifically to determine breathing status per the SIEVE algorithm.
  • Position for breathing — recovery position where appropriate, to maintain a patent airway without further intervention.
  • Move ambulant, uninjured or minorly injured patients out of danger and toward the Survivor Reception Area, freeing the Incident Site Supervisor's attention for higher-acuity patients.

What NOT to Do During Triage SIEVE

  • Do not perform full spinal immobilisation on every patient — this consumes disproportionate time and equipment relative to benefit at this phase.
  • Do not undertake detailed secondary surveys or full histories — the SIEVE is a rapid physiological snapshot only.
  • Do not perform extended wound care, splinting of non-life-threatening fractures, or cosmetic dressing.
  • Do not commit to prolonged single-patient resuscitation (e.g. extended CPR) at the expense of triaging other salvageable patients — this is the practical application of the Expectant/Black category and the "greatest good for the greatest number" principle.
  • Do not establish IV access, administer analgesia, or provide other treatments as a routine part of SIEVE — these belong at the Casualty Clearing Station once SORT re-triage and resourcing allow.
  • Do not allow triage to stall while awaiting a "complete" assessment — an incomplete SIEVE across many patients produces a better system-level outcome than a complete assessment of only a few.

This deliberately austere approach is the aspect of MCI practice most in tension with routine paramedic training, and is precisely why triage command and discipline (rather than individual clinical instinct) governs decision-making on scene.

Chemical, Biological, Radiological, or Nuclear (CBRN) and Hazardous Materials (HAZMAT)

Definitions

HAZMAT: Accidental release of a toxic substance and/or ingestion (accidental or deliberate) of these substances.

CBRN: The deliberate release of a toxic substance to produce mass casualties and panic.

This is the key operational distinction: HAZMAT incidents are (usually) accidental and industrial in origin; CBRN incidents are (by definition) deliberate acts, which carries implications for forensic evidence preservation, secondary device risk, and Police primacy.

Types of Agent

CategorySub-types / Examples
Chemical AgentsNerve agents, blood agents, choking agents, blister agents
Biological AgentsBacterial (e.g. Anthrax), Viral (e.g. Smallpox), Toxin (e.g. Ricin or Botulinum)
Radiological AgentsIonising radiation sources / dispersal devices

Governance

NSW Health Services Functional Area Supporting Plan (HEALTHPLAN); NSW Ambulance Major Incident Response Plan (AMPLAN); the HAZMAT/CBR Emergency Sub Plan (a sub-plan to the State Emergency Management Plan, EMPLAN).

Recognising a CBRN Incident

Primary indicators (recognised immediately by crew):

  • Symptoms experienced by crew themselves on arrival
  • Similar symptoms occurring across multiple patients simultaneously
  • Suspicious items — dissemination devices, warnings left at scene, or claims of responsibility

Secondary indicators (may only become apparent on closer/later inspection):

  • Dead animals or birds in the area
  • Statements made by victims
  • Unusual observations — unexplained liquids, gases, or odours

Initial responders may not recognise or observe indicators of CBRN at all — maintaining a baseline index of suspicion, especially for unexplained multi-patient collapse, is essential.

Management — Key Objectives and "Do Nots"

Key objectives: minimise/prevent exposure and agent injuries; prevent exacerbation through decontamination; control the speed of contamination spread; continue primary medical care objectives once safe to do so.

Do NOT:

  • Do not become a victim
  • Do not rush in
  • Do not assume (the nature or extent of the agent)
  • Do not test — never taste, touch, or smell an unknown substance

General CBRN / HAZMAT Approach

Personal Safety

  • Immediately evacuate to a safe distance, ideally upwind
  • Cover nose and mouth with a damp cloth if no respiratory protection is available
  • Take frequent, shallow breaths
  • Notify Control Centre of a suspected CBRN incident

Decontamination

  • Remove the outer layer of clothing
  • Wash off contamination with water and soap — do NOT scrub, and do NOT use hot water (both can drive agent into the skin or increase absorption)
  • Report to the Fire & Rescue decontamination station

Establishing the Incident Site

  • Remain in the cold zone unless specifically trained and equipped for hot/warm zone entry
  • Report to the Forward Commander

Hot, Warm, and Cold Zones

Hot Zone

  • Area of known contamination
  • Specialist PPE required (Special Operations Team / Fire & Rescue only)
  • Controlled by Fire & Rescue HAZMAT; marked by HAZMAT tape
  • Priority: patient rescue, rapid triage, rapid treatment and evacuation

Warm Zone

  • Area immediately surrounding the hot zone
  • The decontamination area / corridor
  • Controlled by Fire & Rescue HAZMAT; specialist PPE required (SOT/FR only)
  • Priority: patient decontamination, further treatment, post-decontamination triage
  • Other functions: preparation area for hot-zone deployment; crime scene evidence collection point; emergency safe haven

Cold Zone

  • Area cleared of contamination
  • Non-specialist crews permitted
  • Location of the Ambulance Forward Commander
  • Controlled by Police
  • Functions as the staging and treatment area

Surrounding the three zones sits the overall Area of Operations, including the Multi-Agency Incident Management Team and FRNSW Forward Command, with Staging, an Evacuation Assembly Point, an Assembly Area, and a Rehabilitation point positioned outside the cold zone boundary. Entry and exit to the hot zone both route through the decontamination corridor in the warm zone.

CBRN and Triage

The SMART Tag kit includes 20 dedicated Contamination/CBRN tags, distinct from the standard triage tags, allowing contaminated casualties to be clearly identified and tracked through decontamination and subsequent care — the tag remains attached to the casualty throughout the decontamination process.

Managing Bystanders, Other Agencies, and Terrain

Bystanders and Spontaneous Volunteers

  • Rapidly distinguish patients from uninjured bystanders, self-presenting "worried well," and spontaneous volunteers — each requires a different pathway.
  • Direct ambulant, uninjured or minimally affected people to the Survivor Reception Area, where social and mental health workers can support them — this both provides appropriate care and keeps the Incident Site clear for triage and treatment.
  • Spontaneous (untrained) volunteers should never be permitted into the hot or warm zone, or into any area requiring specialist PPE. Where safe and useful, they can be given simple, supervised tasks (e.g. crowd control assistance, guiding walking wounded) under the direction of a uniformed responder — otherwise, they should be excluded from the operational area entirely for their own safety and to avoid scene congestion.
  • Be alert to secondary self-presentation — patients who leave the scene under their own power and later present to a health facility may not be captured in the initial casualty count, and hospitals should be alerted to expect this.
  • Where the incident is also a crime scene (terrorism, criminal act), preserve evidence: avoid unnecessary disturbance of the environment, note (but do not necessarily act on) anything that appears to be evidence, and recognise that Police control of the Body Holding Area and broader scene forensic requirements may constrain otherwise-routine paramedic practice.

Working With Other Agencies

  • Unified command under the AIIMS framework (management by objectives, functional structures, span of control) is what allows Police, Fire & Rescue, SES, Ambulance, and supporting agencies to operate on one incident without duplicating or contradicting each other.
  • Respect designated combat agency primacy for the incident type (e.g. Police for terrorism and major transport incidents; Fire & Rescue for HAZMAT and structure collapse) — this determines who holds overall scene authority even while NSW Ambulance retains clinical authority over patient care.
  • Use dedicated, agency-specific and joint liaison channels rather than informal cross-agency communication; report through your own Forward Commander rather than freelancing directly with another agency's personnel.
  • Liaise closely at zone boundaries — the handover from Fire & Rescue-controlled hot/warm zones to the Ambulance/Police-influenced cold zone is a frequent point of confusion if not deliberately managed.
  • Recognise the value of forward liaison officers from each agency co-located at (or near) the Forward Command Post to maintain a shared operating picture.

Working in Difficult Environments and Terrain

  • Rural, remote, or off-road incidents may significantly delay access, extend transport times, and require 4WD or specialist vehicle access, air transport, or extended extrication.
  • Uneven, unstable, or confined terrain constrains where the Staging Area, CCS, and Loading Point can practically be established — flexibility in scene layout is often required.
  • Extended operations expose responders to environmental extremes (heat, cold, weather exposure) over prolonged periods; this is a genuine risk to responder safety and performance, managed through the Rehabilitation function and planned crew rotation.
  • Extended PPE wear (especially in HAZMAT SOT-level protection) causes fatigue, heat stress, and reduced dexterity — time-limited deployments with mandatory rehabilitation cycles are standard practice.
  • Night operations require specific planning — lighting for the Incident Site and CCS, and the light sticks included in the SMART kit for low-light patient identification.
  • Difficult terrain increases the risk of responders themselves becoming casualties (falls, entrapment, structural collapse) — reinforcing the "do not become a casualty" principle as an active, ongoing assessment rather than a one-off check on arrival.

Appropriate Treatment & Destinations for Definitive Care

Appropriate Treatment by Phase

Treatment scope shifts deliberately as a patient moves through the system:

  • Incident Site (Triage SIEVE): immediate life-saving interventions only (see dedicated section above) — haemorrhage control and airway opening.
  • Casualty Clearing Station (Triage SORT): more complete assessment and treatment matched to the re-triaged priority category, preparation for transport, and ongoing reassessment as resources allow.
  • Loading Point / Transport: maintenance of treatment already established, with focus on safe packaging and handover documentation rather than initiating new interventions.

Destination by Triage Category

Triage LabelDestination
Red (Priority 1)Major Trauma Centre
Yellow (Priority 2)Hospital appropriate to the patient's condition
Green (Priority 3)An Extended Care Paramedic pathway within the green zone, or a facility not receiving Red or Yellow patients (to preserve trauma centre and major hospital capacity)

The Loading Point Supervisor allocates and documents each transport (patient, vehicle, destination, time) and ensures crews do not deviate from the assigned destination — uncoordinated hospital selection by individual crews can rapidly overwhelm a single facility while others remain under-utilised. Transport modes may include road ambulance, bus (for larger numbers of ambulant patients), fixed-wing, and rotary-wing aircraft, coordinated between the Staging Area and Loading Point.

Clinical Case Analysis & Reflective Practice

Case 1: Rural School Bus Rollover, Entrapment, Poor Access

Presentation: A school bus has rolled on an unsealed rural road, 45 minutes from the nearest base hospital. Multiple students trapped; mobile phone reception is poor; parents are already arriving at the scene.

  • Windscreen and METHANE: The first crew delivers a windscreen report noting the rollover, apparent multiple casualties, difficult terrain, and uncertain communications before committing further — this early warning allows Control to begin mobilising additional resources while the fuller METHANE report (including precise location via GPS coordinates or what3words, given the rural setting) is compiled.
  • Terrain challenges: Unsealed road access may delay heavier rescue and additional ambulance resources; a Helicopter Landing Site may need to be identified early given transport time to a Major Trauma Centre.
  • Bystander management: Arriving parents are a key group requiring active management — they are neither patients nor useful volunteers in a rescue context, and should be directed to a designated area away from the Incident Site (an early, informal Survivor/Family Reception point) to prevent scene congestion and secondary trauma from witnessing extrication.
  • Triage discipline: With likely multiple entrapped patients, SIEVE triage must proceed even where a single trapped child appears most distressing — the greatest-good principle requires identifying all patients' categories before committing extended resources to any one extrication.

Case 2: Suspected Chemical Release at a Shopping Centre

Presentation: Multiple patients at a shopping centre food court present simultaneously with eye irritation, difficulty breathing, and nausea. No obvious trauma. Crew members also begin to feel mild throat irritation on arrival.

  • Recognition: Similar symptoms across multiple, otherwise unconnected patients, plus symptoms in crew themselves, are both primary indicators of a possible CBRN/HAZMAT event — this should immediately trigger the "do not rush in" principle.
  • Immediate action: Evacuate to a safe distance, ideally upwind; notify Control of a suspected CBRN/HAZMAT incident; do not attempt to identify the substance by taste, touch, or smell.
  • Zone establishment: Fire & Rescue HAZMAT will establish and control the hot and warm zones (decontamination corridor); Ambulance remains in the cold zone, with the Forward Commander positioned there and Police controlling overall cold zone access.
  • Triage adaptation: Decontaminated patients are triaged (and tagged with a CBRN/Contamination tag alongside their standard SMART tag) after passing through the warm zone decontamination corridor — treatment is deliberately sequenced after decontamination to avoid spreading contamination into the CCS or transporting vehicles.

Case 3: Reversing Clinical Priorities Under Resource Constraint

Presentation: At a multi-vehicle crash with eight patients and only two crews on scene for the first fifteen minutes, one entrapped patient is in cardiac arrest, while three other patients are conscious with reversible airway obstruction and catastrophic limb haemorrhage.

  • Clinical reasoning: In routine single-patient practice, cardiac arrest commands full resuscitative effort. In a genuine resource-limited MCI, a patient in cardiac arrest — particularly if entrapped — may be triaged as Black/Expectant if extraction and resuscitation would consume disproportionate time relative to the number of other salvageable patients awaiting simple, rapid, life-saving interventions.
  • Application: The two crews instead prioritise catastrophic haemorrhage control (tourniquets) and airway-opening manoeuvres across the other patients, completing a SIEVE of the whole scene before committing to any single, resource-intensive intervention.
  • Command authority: This decision is made under the direction of the Incident Site Supervisor / Forward Commander as a system-level triage decision, not an individual crew's unilateral choice — reinforcing why triage command and documentation matter as much as the clinical skill itself.
