1. The Case for Participatory Action Research & Community-Based Risk Assessment

The Problem with "Top-Down" Assessment

Traditional needs and risk assessments are often extractive: an outside "expert" arrives, collects data from a community, leaves, and analyses it elsewhere. This model has well-documented weaknesses:

  • It privileges the outsider's framing of the "problem", which may not match what the community actually experiences as urgent or important.
  • It treats community members as passive subjects providing data, rather than as holders of expert local knowledge about their own environment, hazards, and coping strategies.
  • It produces low ownership of findings, poor uptake of recommendations, and can reproduce colonial and top-down power relationships between researchers/services and marginalised populations.
  • It misses local, informal knowledge systems (seasonal patterns, informal support networks, safe/unsafe locations) that only become visible when residents are treated as co-investigators rather than respondents.

Participatory Action Research (PAR) & Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR)

Participatory Action Research and Community-Based Participatory Research emerged from work in the "majority world" from the 1970s onward, drawing on Paulo Freire's education for critical consciousness and Robert Chambers' participatory rural appraisal tradition. The guiding shift is research done "with" people, not "on" them — community members become co-researchers who help define the problem, choose methods, collect and interpret data, and decide how findings are used.

In humanitarian, disaster, and public health settings, this same logic underpins community-based risk assessment: rather than an agency unilaterally mapping hazards, local residents walk the ground, draw the maps, and rank the risks themselves, because they are the ones who know which paths flood first, which wells run dry, and which households are least able to evacuate.

Why It Matters — The Practical Payoff

  • Accuracy: Local knowledge fills gaps that outside data collection alone cannot reach (informal settlements, undocumented populations, seasonal hazards).
  • Relevance: Priorities are defined by the people who live the problem, not assumed by the agency doing the assessing.
  • Ownership & sustainability: Communities who help produce the findings are far more likely to act on them and sustain any resulting intervention.
  • Equity: Deliberately engaging marginalised, migrant, disabled, and minority groups counters the tendency of top-down assessment to miss exactly the populations at highest risk.
  • Triangulation: Participatory tools cross-check and enrich data gathered through surveys, GIS, or clinical records, rather than replacing them.

2. Key Frameworks & Core Principles

Participatory Action Research (PAR) Cycle

PAR is often described as an umbrella term for action-oriented participatory research. It is iterative and cyclical rather than linear, moving through four repeating phases:

PhaseWhat Happens
1. Explore / PlanCo-production of the problem statement and possible solutions with co-researchers.
2. ActImplementation of the agreed action plan in the selected setting.
3. ObserveUse of evaluation tools (ethnography, action logs, photo-elicitation, coverage data) to monitor implementation.
4. ReflectJoint reflection with co-researchers and stakeholders on what worked, feeding back into the next cycle.

Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) Partnership Cycle

CBPR is a more structured, long-term collaborative partnership model (Detroit Community-Academic Urban Research Center) in which the community is involved at every stage, not just data collection:

  1. Forming a CBPR partnership
  2. Assessing community strengths and dynamics
  3. Identifying priority public health issues and research questions
  4. Designing and conducting the research (etiologic, intervention, and/or policy)
  5. Feeding back and interpreting findings with the community
  6. Disseminating and translating findings
  7. Maintaining, sustaining, and evaluating the partnership itself

Ten Core CBPR Principles (Israel et al., 2017)

  • Recognises the community as a unit of identity
  • Balances research and action for the mutual benefit of all partners
  • Uses a cyclical, iterative development process
  • Builds on existing community strengths and resources
  • Facilitates equitable, power-sharing partnerships across all research phases
  • Promotes co-learning and capacity building among all partners
  • Disseminates findings to, and with, all partners
  • Emphasises public health problems of genuine local relevance
  • Requires a long-term commitment to sustainability
  • Explicitly addresses race, ethnicity, racism, social class, and cultural humility

Core Values That Underpin Every Method

ConceptWhat It Means in Practice
Co-researchersCommunity members who take on roles across the process — participants, co-facilitators, decision-makers, co-analysts, or co-authors — rather than passive subjects.
PositionalityHow a person's identity (insider/outsider, powerful/powerless, privileged/disadvantaged) shapes how they see and are seen in the research relationship.
ReflexivityOngoing, deliberate reflection by researchers on their own assumptions, biases, and power, often through reflective journalling or team debriefs.
Power dynamicsUnequal power between researcher and community can quickly reduce "participation" to tokenism if not actively managed and shared.
DisseminationFindings are shared back to the community in accessible, meaningful formats (exhibitions, booklets, local-language summaries) — not just in academic papers.

Risk of tokenism: When pre-existing power imbalances are not actively managed, "participation" can become a box-ticking exercise that borrows community members' presence without genuinely sharing decision-making power — particularly damaging for groups already marginalised (people with disability, migrants, residents of informal settlements).

3. Semi-Structured Interviews & Focus Group Discussions

Semi-Structured / Key Informant Interviews

A one-on-one conversation guided by a flexible set of open-ended questions rather than a rigid script, allowing the interviewer to follow points of interest as they arise. Key informant interviews target individuals with particular knowledge (a community leader, a health worker, a long-term resident) rather than a random sample.

How to conduct: Develop a short topic guide rather than a fixed questionnaire; obtain informed consent; build rapport before moving to sensitive questions; use open prompts ("Tell me about...", "What happened when...?"); audio-record and transcribe where consented; probe rather than lead.

Focus Group Discussions (FGDs)

A facilitated discussion with a small group (typically 6–10 participants) who share a relevant characteristic (age, gender, occupation, shared experience), used to explore shared or contested community perspectives and social norms rather than individual biography.

How to conduct: Recruit a homogeneous group to reduce power differentials within the room (e.g. separate groups for older men, younger women); use a skilled facilitator plus a note-taker; open with a low-stakes warm-up activity; use prompts that invite disagreement and discussion, not just agreement; manage dominant voices actively.

Comparing the Two Methods

FeatureSemi-Structured InterviewFocus Group Discussion
Best forIndividual, detailed, or sensitive narrativesGroup norms, shared beliefs, rapid consensus-building
Depth vs breadthHigh depth, one perspective at a timeModerate depth, multiple perspectives at once
EfficiencyTime-intensive per participantMore perspectives gathered per hour of fieldwork
Key riskInterviewer bias/leading questionsGroupthink; dominant voices silencing others
Sensitive topicsBetter suited (privacy, confidentiality)Riskier — participants may self-censor in front of peers

Challenges & Ethical Considerations

  • Power differentials between interviewer and participant (e.g. uniformed clinician vs. patient/community member) can suppress honest disclosure.
  • Recruitment bias: those who volunteer for interviews/FGDs may not represent the most marginalised or hardest-to-reach community members.
  • Confidentiality is harder to guarantee in a group setting than a one-to-one interview.
  • Interpreter or translation needs must be planned for in multilingual communities to avoid losing nuance.

4. Participant Observation

What It Is

An ethnographic method in which the researcher spends extended time embedded within the community or setting being studied, observing (and sometimes participating in) everyday practices as they naturally unfold, rather than relying solely on what people say they do. It captures the gap between reported behaviour and actual behaviour.

How to conduct: Negotiate access and consent with gatekeepers and participants; decide the degree of participation (complete observer through to full participant); keep detailed field notes recorded as soon as practicable after observation; triangulate observations against interviews or documents; maintain a reflexive journal to track how your presence may be shaping what you see.

Strengths & Challenges

StrengthsChallenges
Captures context, routine, and unspoken practices that interviews cannot access; builds deep trust and rapport over time; reveals discrepancies between stated and actual behaviour. Time-intensive; the observer's presence can alter behaviour (the "observer effect"); raises complex positionality questions about being simultaneously insider and outsider; note-taking and recall are imperfect; ethically sensitive if observation is covert.

Relevance to Paramedic Practice

Paramedics are, in a sense, natural participant observers: home visits, community paramedicine clinics, and welfare checks place clinicians directly inside a patient's living environment. An ethnographic evaluation of a rural Ontario community paramedicine program used exactly this combination — informal discussion, semi-structured interviews, and direct observation of paramedic-consumer interactions — to understand consumer experience of the service in a way that a survey alone could not capture.

5. Transect Walks

What It Is

A systematic walk along a defined route through a community, conducted jointly by researchers and community members, to observe, ask, listen, and record conditions relevant to the research question (water and sanitation infrastructure, hazards, community structures, service gaps). The route is ideally a straight-line cross-section that captures the greatest diversity of features, though a practical path is often substituted. Findings are recorded as a transect diagram or grid.

How to Conduct a Transect Walk

  1. Identify key informants/community members from all relevant stakeholder groups who are willing to walk and share observations.
  2. Agree the purpose and route with participants; maps or aerial imagery can help but are not essential.
  3. Agree a short list of parameters to observe (five or six maximum — too many causes confusion).
  4. Walk the route, stopping at defined observation points to record all parameters; ask questions of anyone encountered along the way.
  5. After the walk, draw a diagram or grid and hold a full discussion with participants to interpret findings.
  6. If the area is large, split the transect into segments covered by small teams, then combine results.

Advantages & Disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Identifies cause-and-effect relationships between features along the route; surfaces problems/opportunities perceived by different stakeholder groups; builds rapid rapport as a "community entry" activity; supports site selection; triangulates data from other methods; can be repeated over time for monitoring and evaluation. Only captures the currently observable situation — an entry point rather than deep analysis; may be impossible to assemble every relevant stakeholder group; typically takes 2–4 hours including write-up, and interest can wane during the diagramming stage; safety of participants must be actively managed in insecure areas.

Application to Community-Based Risk Assessment

Transect walks are widely used as an entry-point tool in humanitarian and disaster risk contexts. A documented example is a transect walk conducted in the Itereleng informal settlement (Tshwane, South Africa) specifically to observe and map community vulnerability — using the walk-and-discuss method to identify hazard exposure, unsafe structures, and populations at greatest risk, in a setting where formal survey data was sparse.

Materials needed: notebook and pen, appropriate clothing/footwear, maps or aerial photos if available, optional GPS device, camera or voice recorder, and — for the write-up — flipchart paper, coloured pens, and coloured cards.

6. Village & Social (Community) Mapping

What It Is

Community members create their own map of the village or neighbourhood — using pens and paper, or locally available materials such as sticks and stones on the ground — to represent structures, resources, hazards, and points of social interaction as they understand them. Different demographic groups (older men, younger women, etc.) are often asked to map separately, then compare, to surface differing perspectives on the same physical space.

How to Conduct

  1. Identify the community groups to participate (consider splitting by gender, age, or occupation to reduce power effects within the group).
  2. Obtain informed consent; provide simple materials.
  3. Ask participants to draw their community, prompting with questions: What is located here? Who meets there, and how often? Where do people collect water or firewood? Where do people pray or gather socially? Where are the points of influence/power in the community?
  4. Use the completed map as a discussion tool: which structures matter for health/safety? What would participants change? Which existing structures could be leveraged for a programme or an emergency response?
  5. Bring separate groups' maps together for comparison and facilitated dialogue, managing power dynamics carefully.

Village Mapping in Practice: A Nigerian Case Example

In a Kaduna State (Nigeria) neglected tropical disease programme, four separate mapping groups (older men, older women, younger men, younger women) each produced a community map to understand low medicine-distribution coverage. The groups identified broadly similar physical structures but attached very different meanings to them — younger women nominated public boreholes as ideal sites for health sensitisation, while younger men nominated football-viewing venues for the same purpose. Comparing the four maps surfaced migrant routes and settlements that had consistently been missed by the existing programme.

Challenges

  • Artistic/literacy confidence can inhibit participation unless facilitators explicitly frame the exercise as expression, not art.
  • Power differentials within a mixed group can mean the map reflects only the most vocal participants' worldview.
  • Maps are a snapshot in time and require repetition to track change.
  • Facilitators need a safe, private space to hold the follow-up discussion, particularly where the map surfaces sensitive information (e.g. locations of vulnerable households).

7. Photovoice

What It Is

Developed by Wang and Burris (1997) from research with women in rural China, photovoice is a form of both participatory action research and CBPR in which participants are given cameras to photograph their own experiences, strengths, and concerns, then use those images to prompt narrative discussion. Its three goals are: (1) enable people to record and reflect on their community's strengths and concerns; (2) promote critical dialogue through group discussion of photographs; and (3) reach policymakers with the resulting themes.

How to Conduct — The SHOWeD Method

  1. Train participants in camera use, ethics of photography, and the research theme.
  2. Participants photograph their community/experiences over an agreed period.
  3. Conduct an individual follow-up interview per participant, using the SHOWeD prompts:
LetterPrompt
SWhat do you See here?
HWhat is really Happening here?
OHow does this relate to Our lives?
WWhy does this situation, concern, or strength exist?
DWhat can we Do about it?
  1. Participants select 10–20 key images that represent recurring themes.
  2. In groups, participants present, discuss, and agree captions for the selected images.
  3. Images are shared in an exhibition or booklet with stakeholders/policymakers to prompt action.

Ethical Considerations

  • Informed consent for photography must be explicit, revisited throughout the project, and never coerced — especially where the population is already stigmatised.
  • Faces or identifying features are often blurred, or participants are trained to use symbolism instead of direct portraits, to protect confidentiality and prevent further marginalisation.
  • Managing participant expectations from the outset is critical — photovoice can inform action but does not, by itself, solve the problem depicted.
  • Facilitators need a plan for handling "negative practices" that photographs may inadvertently reveal, without further stigmatising those involved.

Applications

Photovoice has been used to design peer support groups for people affected by stigmatising skin disease in Nigeria, to understand the vulnerability and empowerment of female sex workers engaging with HIV self-testing in Malawi (where participants used symbolism rather than portraits to protect anonymity), and — closer to the original public health application — to help maternal and child health program staff in the United States expand their needs assessments beyond the indicators clinicians typically prioritise (low birth weight, maternal mortality) to the concerns residents themselves identified as most pressing.

8. Reflecting on the Value for Paramedicine Research & Community Engagement

Why This Matters to Paramedics

Paramedicine is increasingly expanding beyond emergency response into community paramedicine, health promotion, and preventative care delivered in patients' homes and community spaces. This shift brings paramedics into sustained contact with the same populations that participatory methods are designed to engage — older residents in social housing, rural and remote communities, and culturally and linguistically diverse or marginalised groups — making CBPR and PAR directly relevant to how paramedic services design, evaluate, and improve community-facing programs.

Case Example: PAR with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Communities

Following the 2009 H1N1 pandemic, which disproportionately affected Indigenous populations across Oceania and the Americas, Miller et al. (2015) used a Participatory Action Research framework to better understand Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community members' own perceptions of pandemic influenza risk. Rather than imposing an external risk model, the PAR approach let community members define what mattered to them — informed by higher rates of comorbidity, but also by institutionalised racism within health services and unequal access to culturally safe care. This is a direct, Australian, health-service example of community-based risk assessment done "with" rather than "on" a population, and a template for how paramedic services might approach risk communication and service design with Indigenous and other marginalised communities.

Case Example: Ethnographic Evaluation of Community Paramedicine

An observational ethnographic evaluation of a rural Ontario community paramedicine program combined informal discussion, semi-structured interviews, and direct observation of paramedic–consumer interactions to understand the lived experience of fourteen consumers across four program components. This is essentially participant observation and interviewing applied to paramedic service evaluation, and produced richer insight into perceived security, health literacy, and trust than a satisfaction survey alone could offer — directly informing the case for expanding community paramedicine models in rural Australia.

Honest Tensions for Paramedic Practice

  • Time and operational pressure: genuine CBPR requires a long-term, sustained partnership (Israel principle 9) — difficult to reconcile with the acute, time-critical culture of frontline EMS. This favours partnership between ambulance services and academic/community health teams over ad hoc individual effort.
  • Power imbalance: a uniformed paramedic, however well-intentioned, carries institutional authority that can suppress honest community disclosure unless actively and visibly shared.
  • Scope of practice: paramedics are clinicians first; embedding formal participatory research requires partnership with trained researchers and appropriate ethics oversight, not ad hoc data collection on shift.
  • Cultural safety: the Paramedicine Board of Australia's capability framework and national strategy both emphasise culturally safe, community-engaged practice — participatory methods are one concrete way to operationalise that expectation rather than leave it as an abstract value.

9. Reflective Practice

Consider these scenarios and questions:

  1. A rural community has repeatedly declined a proposed flood-evacuation plan drawn up by emergency services. Which participatory method(s) would you propose to understand why, and how would you manage the power imbalance between "the service" and "the community"?
  2. You are asked to help design a photovoice project with people who inject drugs to understand barriers to calling emergency services during an overdose. What specific ethical safeguards would this population require beyond the general principles outlined above?
  3. Compare the PAR cycle (explore–act–observe–reflect) with the structured, protocol-driven clinical reasoning paramedics are trained in. Where do these two ways of thinking support each other, and where might they conflict?
  4. Reflect on your own positionality as a paramedicine student and future registered clinician. How might your uniform, institutional affiliation, and clinical authority shape what a community member is willing to tell you in an interview or during a transect walk?
  5. A village mapping exercise conducted separately with men's and women's groups produces two very different maps of "unsafe areas" in the same community. As the facilitator, how would you bring these groups together to discuss the discrepancy without reproducing existing power dynamics?
  6. Israel et al.'s CBPR principles require a long-term commitment to sustainability. What structural changes would an ambulance service need to make to genuinely sustain a CBPR partnership with a community, rather than a one-off consultation?
