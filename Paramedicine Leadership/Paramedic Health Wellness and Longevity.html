LO1: Challenges to Physical and Mental Health in Paramedicine

Why This Matters Beyond the Individual

Ambulance services have to put patients first, but they also need healthy people to deliver that care. Meadley and colleagues argue that a safe and healthy workplace is part of a service's duty of care, not an optional extra. Two things follow:

  • A paramedic who is exhausted, hypervigilant or numb is not at their best for the next patient. Poor health is a patient-safety issue.
  • Illness creates roster gaps. Colleagues fill them with extra shifts, which raises their own exposure and risk. This is the mechanism behind the "downward spiral" later in this section.

The Other Half of the Picture (Important for LO3)

  • Streb et al. begin from the observation that paramedics have higher PTSD rates than the general population, yet the vast majority do not develop PTSD. The research question therefore becomes "what protects?", which is a salutogenic question.
  • In the Catalonian EMS sample, positive mental health scores were high (mean 131.8 on a 39 to 156 scale), and 94% reported job satisfaction.
  • Take-home: paramedicine is a high-risk occupation, not a doomed one. Protective factors are identifiable and modifiable.

Trauma Exposure Is Rarely One Event: "Traumatic Adaptation"

Pronk describes a process he calls traumatic adaptation. The first death, amputation or seriously ill child is a shock. By the tenth or twentieth, it barely registers as unusual, and every colleague is recalibrating in the same way, so nobody has an outside reference point. Meanwhile the cumulative load keeps building (his image is a "psychological bucket" slowly filling).

Check-question he suggests: "Are my experiences unusually traumatic or stressful compared with the average person?" If yes, a preventative conversation with a psychologist is reasonable before symptoms appear.

Appraisal: This is a useful teaching frame from a practitioner, not an established clinical construct. It fits the clinical literature on cumulative exposure, but cite it as opinion.

1.3 Physical Health Challenges

Chronic Stress Physiology in Brief

Threat activates the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary system (adrenaline, heart rate, blood pressure) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (cortisol). That is adaptive for a resus. It becomes costly when it is switched on repeatedly and recovery is incomplete: constant low-level activation, punctuated by acute spikes, as Pronk puts it. The cumulative wear is often called allostatic load.

Repeated activationUnpredictable calls, night work, occupational violence risk.
→
Incomplete recoveryShort sleep, back-to-back jobs, no decompression.
→
Physiological wearRaised blood pressure, insulin resistance, immune and sleep disruption.
→
Clinical outcomesCardiometabolic disease, injury, low mood, anxiety.

The physical and mental pathways feed each other: poor sleep worsens mood, and low mood worsens sleep, eating and activity.

DomainWhat happensEvidence and notes
Shift work, sleep and circadian misalignment Working against the body clock is linked to metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease and mental health conditions. Night shifts add fatigue, sleepiness and stress, and impair performance. Fatigue also makes drowsy driving after a night shift a real hazard. Khan et al. 2020 and 2021 (Australian paramedics). Crowther et al. 2025 followed intern paramedics as they began shift work, and found the transition to shift work is a vulnerable period for sleep, diet and activity.

Together with low activity and shift work, this raises the risk of weight gain, type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Injury from violence and hazards Assault, sharps and infectious exposure, road-crash risk in emergency driving. Pronk 2021 states every paramedic will meet at least verbal aggression, and many will meet physical or sexual assault. Underreporting is common.

1.4 The Spectrum of Mental Health Outcomes

Mental health in paramedicine is not a switch between "fine" and "PTSD". Most paramedics sit somewhere on a continuum and move along it over a career. The conditions below are the ones most often studied in ambulance personnel.

OutcomeCore featuresParamedic-specific context
Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) After exposure to actual or threatened death or serious injury: intrusions (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance, negative changes in mood and thinking, and hyperarousal, lasting longer than a month and impairing function. Can follow a single critical incident (a child death, a colleague injured, a threat to the paramedic's own life) or the accumulation of many exposures. Paramedics are also exposed indirectly through patients and families.
Depression Persistent low mood or loss of interest, with changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration and self-worth. Often overlaps with burnout, chronic fatigue and sleep disorders, which makes it easy to miss or mislabel as "just tired".
Anxiety disorders Excessive worry, restlessness, muscle tension, panic, and avoidance. Includes anticipatory worry before shifts. Pronk calls constant worry about the next job sensitising, which keeps a chronic stress response switched on.
Psychological distress Non-specific symptoms (nervousness, hopelessness, fatigue) that fall short of a diagnosis but predict later illness. The most common finding in prevalence studies (about 27% pooled).
Substance use Alcohol or other drugs used to wind down, sleep, or numb intrusive memories. Named by Pronk and Lawn et al. as a common maladaptive coping strategy, alongside avoidance. Short-term relief, long-term harm, and it impairs sleep.
Suicidal ideation and behaviour Thoughts of ending one's life, planning, or attempts. Estimated about 10% for ideation in paramedic research, and likely under-reported. In the Australian emergency services survey, suicidal thoughts were about twice and plans over three times as common as in the general population. Paramedics are also trained to assess risk in patients, which does not make them immune. Ask directly and early, with someone, when you are worried about a colleague or yourself.

1.5 Risk and Protective Factors

Trauma exposure does not always lead to illness. Streb et al. start from that observation, and the Johnston et al. review concluded that PTSD in paramedics reflects an interplay of occupational, social and personal factors. This is the bridge to LO2 and LO3.

LevelRisk factors (pushing toward illness)Protective factors (pushing toward health)
Individual Poor sleep, avoidance coping, alcohol use, low sense of coherence, external locus of control, prior trauma, rumination Resilience, sense of coherence, internal locus of control, problem-focused coping, competence and confidence (stress inoculation), meaning in the work
Social / team Isolation, stigma, unsupportive peers, strained home life Social support, peer support, workplace belongingness, collaboration across agencies
Organisational Overtime, roster gaps, bullying, unsupportive managers, no debriefing, a reactive support culture Job satisfaction, supportive leadership, fatigue-aware rostering, proactive wellbeing programs

In the Catalonian EMS sample, participants who were fully satisfied at work scored higher on every positive mental health factor, and working collaboratively with other institutions was associated with greater personal satisfaction and interpersonal skills (Mantas-Jiménez et al. 2024). Associations only: the design cannot show that satisfaction causes health.

1.6 Culture, Stigma and Help-Seeking

  • A culture of toughness. Lawn et al. (2020) describe a military-like or "macho" culture that discourages open distress. Paramedics who feel they must look unaffected delay help.
  • Normalisation. When everyone is equally exposed, nobody has an outside reference point (see traumatic adaptation above).
  • Reactive systems. Pronk's critique: employee assistance programs often intervene only after problems are detected, so "the system waits for the paramedic to break." By then there may already be sick leave, a compensation claim and a roster gap.
  • The return-to-the-same-job problem. Treatment that only restores function so the person can return to the same exposures, with no plan to build resilience, leaves the recurrence risk unchanged.
  • Training gap. Surveys cited by Pronk suggest paramedics receive little education on dealing with the stressors of the role in their training. This guide exists partly to close that gap.

Burnout and Related Concepts

What Burnout Is (and Is Not)

Burnout is a response to chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is classified in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, not as a medical condition. It is defined by three dimensions (Maslach and colleagues):

DimensionMeaningWhat it can look like on road
Emotional exhaustion Feeling drained and depleted. The core, "stress" dimension. Dreading the pager, no energy left for family after a shift, tiredness that sleep does not fix.
Depersonalisation / cynicism Increased mental distance from the job and the people in it. A defensive, detached response. Labelling patients ("frequent flyer"), dismissing distress, joking about suffering in a way that has turned bitter.
Reduced personal accomplishment Feeling ineffective and that the work no longer matters. "What's the point, nothing I do changes anything," avoiding learning, losing pride in clinical work.

These dimensions map neatly onto SOC (LO3): a collapse in meaningfulness shows up as cynicism and low accomplishment, and a collapse in manageability shows up as exhaustion.

Why Burnout Develops: The Job Demands-Resources Idea

A widely used explanation is that burnout arises when demands (workload, emotional load, time pressure, aggression) stay high while resources (control over work, support, recovery time, training, recognition) are too low. Two pathways follow:

  • Health-impairment path: chronic high demand without recovery leads to exhaustion and then illness.
  • Motivational path: abundant resources lead to engagement and satisfaction, which buffers the demands.

This is why the organisational tier matters. Individual resilience skills cannot fully compensate for a job design with permanently excessive demands and few resources.

Burnout vs Its Neighbours

Examiners like these distinctions. Use the table to pick the right term and the right response.

ConceptCore ideaTypical triggerKey discriminator
Burnout Exhaustion, cynicism and reduced efficacy from chronic work stress. Sustained workload and organisational factors. Work-related; builds gradually; the job context is the problem.
Compassion fatigue Reduced capacity for empathy after prolonged exposure to others' suffering. Repeated caring for people in pain. The empathic "well" runs dry. Contrasts with compassion satisfaction, the pleasure of helping.
Secondary / vicarious traumatic stress Trauma symptoms from hearing about or witnessing the trauma of others. Indirect exposure to patients' and families' trauma. Symptoms resemble PTSD, but exposure is secondary.
Moral injury Distress after acting, or failing to act, in ways that violate one's moral code, or witnessing it. Care constrained by system limits (long waits, ramping, resource shortages), or a decision that went badly. Core emotions are guilt, shame and anger, rather than fear.
PTSD Fear-based syndrome after traumatic exposure: intrusions, avoidance, negative cognitions, arousal. Specific potentially traumatic events. Requires a traumatic event and a symptom cluster for more than a month. It is a clinical diagnosis.

These conditions overlap and co-occur. A paramedic can be burnt out and also have PTSD, and burnout can progress to depression. Do not use the labels to pigeonhole a colleague. Use the observed behaviours to start a supportive conversation.

What the Paramedic Burnout Evidence Adds

  • Korean paramedic path analysis (n = 208, Busan): post-traumatic stress was most strongly associated with burnout. Workplace violence was associated with burnout indirectly through post-traumatic stress. Resilience reduced post-traumatic stress, which gave it an indirect protective effect on burnout, but it had no direct effect on burnout. Lesson: resilience is not a magic shield, and trauma and violence exposure matter independently.
  • Longitudinal evidence cited by Mantas-Jiménez et al. 2024: a high sense of coherence was linked to a lower risk of burnout symptoms ten years later, and to higher work engagement. Engagement is the positive counterpart of burnout.
  • Mixed occupational groups (medical staff, police, firefighters; BMC Psychiatry 2020): a higher sense of coherence, trait resilience and an internal locus of control were each associated with fewer psychopathological symptoms, less post-traumatic stress and less burnout. An external locus of control showed the opposite pattern.

All are observational. They show association and plausible mechanisms, not proof that raising SOC or internal control lowers burnout. Intervention trials are still limited.

LO2: Locus of Control and Paramedic Practice

Definition

Locus of control (Rotter) is a person's generalised belief about what determines the outcomes in their life. It is a continuum, not a pair of boxes.

External: "What happens is mostly down to luck, fate, other people or the system." Internal: "What happens depends largely on my own actions and choices."
  • Levenson's refinement splits external control into powerful others (managers, doctors, the organisation) and chance (luck, fate), so three dimensions are measured: internality, powerful others, chance.
  • Domain-specific versions exist (work locus of control, health locus of control). A paramedic can feel highly in control of their clinical skills but powerless over their roster.
  • It is an attribution style, not a fixed trait. It is shaped by experience and can shift with training, support and reflection.

2.3 Locus of Control in Paramedic Practice

Paramedic work is a perfect training ground for control paradoxes. You manage high-stakes situations with limited information, and you regularly face outcomes that you cannot change. A healthy practitioner learns to separate three zones.

Circles of Control, Influence and Concern

  • Control: my clinical technique, preparation, study, sleep routine, how I respond to a bad job, who I talk to.
  • Influence: crew dynamics, team culture, how a handover lands, patient decisions, rostering feedback, union and WHS channels.
  • Concern (no direct control): whether a patient survives, the number of calls, hospital ramping, other people's behaviour.

Effort is best spent on the inner circles. Energy spent ruminating on the outer ring drains the resources needed for the inner ones.

Process Orientation vs Outcome Orientation

A paramedic can control the quality of the process (assessment, protocol adherence, communication, compassion) but not always the outcome (the patient may die despite excellent care). Anchoring self-worth to outcomes you cannot control feeds guilt, burnout and moral injury. Anchoring it to the process you can control supports an internal locus of control that is accurate rather than inflated.

2.5 Related Concepts (to Avoid Muddling in Exams)

ConceptMeaningRelationship to locus of control
Self-efficacy (Bandura) Belief in one's ability to perform a specific task successfully. Locus of control is about whether outcomes depend on me. Self-efficacy is about whether I can do the behaviour. You can believe outcomes depend on effort and still doubt your skill.
Learned helplessness (Seligman) Passivity after repeated experiences of uncontrollable events. A strongly external orientation, acquired through experience. Long periods of futile effort (for example, constant ramping) can teach it.
Stress inoculation Reduced stress reaction to a task through repeated, successful exposure and competence (Pronk's IV-cannulation example). Builds self-efficacy and with it a sense of control, which supports an internal orientation.
Sense of coherence See LO3. Related but not the same. Salutogenesis does not treat a sense of control as the primary or necessary feature of health.

Critical Appraisal: Is "More Internal" Always Better?

For an analytically rigorous answer, avoid the slogan "internal good, external bad".

  • Realism matters. The healthiest position is accurate appraisal of what is and is not controllable. Believing you control uncontrollable outcomes invites guilt, hindsight bias and moral injury ("I should have saved them").
  • Some external attributions are correct. Hospital ramping, unsafe rosters and understaffing are real structural problems. Attributing them to the system is accurate, and the healthy action is to report and advocate (influence), not to absorb them.
  • Responsibilisation risk. An over-emphasis on personal resilience can quietly shift responsibility from employers to individuals. Employers carry duties under work health and safety law, including managing psychosocial hazards. Meadley et al. frame paramedic wellbeing as an employer duty of care as well as an individual task.
  • Pronk's framing is opinion. His claim that external locus of control leads to a "victim mentality" is a persuasive argument, not a research finding, and it should not be used to blame someone who is struggling.
  • Direction of cause is uncertain. Burnout and trauma may also lower a person's sense of control.

Why It Matters for Patients Too

Patients have a locus of control as well. A patient with COPD who believes "nothing I do makes a difference" is less likely to use an action plan, take medication or seek help early. Respectful paramedic practice supports patient autonomy: explain, offer choices, and link self-management and referral pathways (community paramedicine, GP, chronic disease services). Your own sense of agency and your ability to foster it in patients are connected.

LO3: Salutogenesis and Sense of Coherence

Two Questions About Health

Pathogenesis (the dominant medical model)Salutogenesis (Antonovsky)
Central question "What causes disease, and how do we remove it?" "What creates and sustains health, and why do some people stay well despite stress?"
View of health Healthy or diseased (a dichotomy). A continuum from "health ease" to "dis-ease". Everyone sits somewhere on it and can move.
View of stressors Stressors are pathogenic and should be eliminated. Stressors are ubiquitous. The outcome depends on how tension is managed, and can be neutral or even health-promoting.
Focus Risk factors, deficits and treatment. Resources, strengths and assets: general resistance resources and a sense of coherence.
Paramedic example Screening for PTSD and treating it once it is present. Asking why most paramedics do not develop PTSD, and building those protective factors in everyone.

Salutogenesis and pathogenesis are complementary, not rivals. Psychological First Aid is an illness-prevention approach after exposure to stressful events, whereas SOC is a health-promoting concept that builds positive mental health (Mantas-Jiménez et al. 2024). Good workplace programs use both.

Origins (Commonly Recounted Background)

Aaron Antonovsky, a medical sociologist, introduced the concept in the late 1970s (Health, Stress and Coping, 1979) and developed it in Unraveling the Mystery of Health (1987). His starting puzzle: given how many stressors everyone encounters, why do some people stay healthy and keep a positive outlook while others do not? It is commonly recounted that he noticed this while studying women's adaptation to menopause, where a subset of Holocaust survivors in the sample were coping well. The SOC concept was his answer.

GRRs are the assets that help people deal with stressors. 3.2 Sense of Coherence: Definition

Sense of coherence (SOC) is a global, non-specific orientation to life: a person's enduring tendency to see the world as comprehensible, manageable and meaningful. It is the core of Antonovsky's theory. Riopel describes it as a mix of optimism and a sense of control, and notes that Antonovsky did not regard control itself as the central or necessary feature. A strong SOC lets people use the resources in their environment effectively, which supports wellbeing (Mantas-Jiménez et al. 2024).

3.6 Criticisms and Limits of SOC

  • Dimensionality. Antonovsky treated SOC as a single global construct, but later work suggests it is multidimensional (Eriksson and Mittelmark).
  • Predictability assumption. The SOC-29 includes several items that assume a predictable inner and outer world. Some researchers argue unpredictability can energise people and is not necessarily unhealthy, which matters in an unpredictable job like paramedicine.
  • Physical health. The SOC-29 and SOC-13 are only moderately related to measures of physical health. The evidence is strongest for mental health and quality of life.
  • Overlap with mood. Some critics argue SOC overlaps with negative affect and depressive symptoms, which would inflate its link with mental health. This is a standard caveat for self-report scales.
  • Stability. Antonovsky thought SOC became relatively stable by about age 30 and declined after retirement. Later empirical work does not support the decline and suggests SOC keeps developing, and may increase with age. SOC appears relatively stable but modifiable.
  • Design. Most studies are cross-sectional. We know that SOC and good mental health go together, not that raising SOC raises health. Longitudinal studies exist, such as burnout risk ten years later, but intervention evidence in paramedics is limited.

B. For Your Patients

  • Comprehensibility: explain what is happening and what will happen next in plain language.
  • Manageability: identify the patient's resources (family, GP, medications, services) and connect them to missing ones. Offer choices where safe to do so.
  • Meaningfulness: ask what matters most to them, and anchor care and decisions to it, especially in palliative and end-of-life care.

C. For Teams and Leaders

  • Comprehensibility: predictable, transparent communication about changes and decisions.
  • Manageability: workloads, staffing, equipment and support that match demands, and early access to help.
  • Meaningfulness: recognition, feedback loops on patient outcomes, and alignment between daily work and professional values. Mantas-Jiménez et al. highlight that managers in emergency services play a key role in shaping work environments that promote positive mental health.

LO4: Strategies for Health, Resilience and Longevity

Framing: Resilience Is a Modifiable Process, Not a Trait

Pronk's Resilience Shield model pictures resilience and stress as opposite pans of a set of scales. In paramedicine the scales are tipped toward stress by the nature of the job, and the usual organisational response only acts after they tip over. The alternative is to build resilience proactively and daily, so that the scales are rebalanced before an injury occurs. This matches the PHAWRU aim of producing evidence-based strategies to improve musculoskeletal, physiological, metabolic, nutritional, mental and sleep health (Meadley et al. 2020), and the recommendation of Mantas-Jiménez et al. Clinical Case Analysis and Critical Reasoning

Work through each scenario before opening the model answer. Aim to name the relevant concept, apply LO1 to LO4, and justify an action.

Case 1: The Graduate After a Paediatric Arrest

Presentation: A graduate paramedic, six months in, attended an unsuccessful paediatric cardiac arrest last week. Since then they are sleeping badly, replaying the job at night, and keep saying, "If I had gone for the airway sooner, it might have been different." They are avoiding the cot-death scenario in the pre-shift briefing and have told nobody. They say they are "fine."

Model analysis
  • Normal reaction or concern? Distress, poor sleep and intrusive memories in the first days to weeks after a traumatic event are common and often settle. Persisting symptoms beyond about a month, avoidance and functional impact are the signal for professional assessment. This is at the reacting end (yellow zone), with risk of moving toward orange if avoidance and isolation continue.
  • Locus of control: the statement shows an over-internal attribution for an outcome that may not have been controllable. The aim is accurate appraisal: review the process with a clinical lead, using evidence on paediatric arrest outcomes, rather than self-blame.
  • SOC: comprehensibility is low (replaying the job, searching for meaning), manageability is at risk (nobody told), and meaningfulness may be threatened if the grief turns into "this work is too much."
  • Actions: a supportive, voluntary conversation with a peer or supervisor, clinical review for learning, peer support or employee assistance, sleep protection and routine maintenance, a plan to check in again in a few days and again at about a month, and the GP or psychologist if symptoms persist. Normalise help-seeking as professional practice.
  • Avoid: forced emotional debriefing, "you did everything right, don't think about it," or leaving the person alone because they said they are fine (stigma and under-reporting).

Case 2: Four Nights, Five Hours' Sleep

Presentation: A paramedic with three years' experience works a block of four consecutive night shifts. They sleep about five hours between shifts in a bright bedroom, use two energy drinks a night, eat takeaway at 3 am, and have two or three beers after the last shift to "switch off." On the drive home they had a near-miss from a micro-sleep.

Model analysis
  • Hazards (LO1): circadian misalignment, sleep restriction, high caffeine, late heavy meals, alcohol (which fragments sleep), and fatigue-related driving risk. Khan et al. report that night shifts raise stress, fatigue and sleepiness, and paramedics have poorer sleep quality than reference populations.
  • Immediate priority: safety. A micro-sleep is a red flag. Do not drive home drowsy: nap first, take a break, or arrange transport. Report the fatigue event through the service's fatigue process.
  • Control and influence (LO2): in control of the sleep environment, caffeine timing, food choices, alcohol, and a pre-shift nap. Influence over the roster is through reporting and advocacy.
  • Strategies (LO4): blackout curtains and a cool, quiet room, a consistent main sleep, an early-night-shift nap, caffeine only early in the shift, lighter meals in the small hours with a planned snack, a non-alcoholic wind-down ritual, and alcohol within guidelines. Review the pattern with a GP if sleep problems persist (for example, snoring or suspected sleep apnoea).
  • Organisation: consecutive night limits, adequate rest between blocks, and access to a safe place to rest after shift.

Case 3: The Cynical Partner

Presentation: You are crewed with a colleague of twelve years' experience who has changed. They sigh at every call, refer to patients as "time-wasters," joke darkly about deaths in a way that has turned bitter, have called in sick more often, and mention drinking most nights "to sleep." They say, "I'm just over it."

Model analysis
  • Concept: the pattern fits burnout (exhaustion, depersonalisation or cynicism, reduced accomplishment), with a risk of depression and substance use. Do not diagnose. Describe the behaviours you have seen.
  • SOC lens: meaningfulness has collapsed, and manageability (exhaustion, alcohol) is failing.
  • Approach: Ask (private, specific, caring), Listen without fixing, Encourage action (GP, peer support, employee assistance), Check in. Offer to help them make the first call.
  • Ask directly about suicidal thoughts if you are worried, because asking does not increase risk. If there is immediate danger, call 000. Otherwise, make sure they know of Lifeline (13 11 14) and the Suicide Call Back Service (1300 659 467).
  • Patient safety and professional obligations: if their impairment is affecting patient care (for example, being drunk at work or errors), you must escalate through the proper channels. Confidentiality has limits. Concerns about fitness to practise are handled through your service's processes and professional obligations.
  • Avoid: joining the cynicism, minimising ("we all feel like that"), or covering for unsafe behaviour indefinitely.

Case 4: Assaulted on Scene, "Part of the Job"

Presentation: A crew is punched and threatened by an intoxicated patient. Both want to carry on; the paramedic who was hit has a sore jaw and is shaky. A supervisor says, "It's part of the job. Don't make a fuss with a report."

Model analysis
  • Why this matters (LO1): occupational violence is common, under-reported, and linked to post-traumatic stress and, in a Korean path analysis, indirectly to burnout. Normalising it feeds the culture of silence.
  • Immediate care: make the scene safe, call for police support, assess and treat injuries (jaw, head injury screen), and offer the person time and a break before the next job.
  • Locus of control (LO2): this is a case where an external attribution is accurate: the behaviour was the patient's and the safety system is the organisation's. The internal and influence response is to report, seek medical review, use available support, and use the incident process and union or WHS channels if the supervisor pushes back. Avoid self-blame ("I should have seen it coming").
  • SOC (LO3): a prompt, supported response strengthens comprehensibility (this was a known hazard, the system responds) and manageability (help is available).
  • Organisational duty (LO4): managers have duties to manage the risk, record and investigate incidents, and support affected staff. "Part of the job" is not an acceptable control.
  • Peer and personal: peer check-in later that shift and a few days after, and keep an eye out for hypervigilance, anger or avoidance.

Case 5: Applying SOC to a Patient

Presentation: You attend an 82-year-old woman who lives alone and has had three falls this month. She is frightened and says, "I'm just a burden. It's no use." Her daughter, who is overwhelmed, says she cannot keep up. There are no acute injuries.

Model analysis
  • Pathogenic approach: identify injuries and cause of falls (medical assessment, medications, orthostatic changes, hazards). Necessary.
  • Salutogenic addition: ask what is going well and what matters to her.
    • C Explain clearly what you think is happening and what the options are.
    • M Identify and link resources: GP review, falls clinic, home safety assessment, community services, carer support for the daughter.
    • ME Explore what makes her days worthwhile (independence, her garden, her grandchildren) and keep decisions tied to those goals.
  • Locus of control: "It's no use" signals an external, helpless orientation. Offering choices and small, achievable steps supports a sense of agency.
  • Disposition: follow local falls and community referral pathways (non-transport options are appropriate for some patients). Document and handover clearly.
  • For you: repeated social-welfare presentations can erode meaningfulness. Linking people to sustained support is one way to keep it intact.

Viva / OSCE-Style Prompts With Key Points

Q1. Explain why paramedics are at greater risk of mental health problems than the general population.

Key points: cumulative and unpredictable trauma exposure; occupational violence; shift work and fatigue; time pressure and system strain; stigma and a culture of toughness; little training and reactive support. Quote the prevalence figures with caveats (PTSD about 11%, depression and anxiety about 15%, distress about 27%, probably under-reported). Add that most paramedics do not develop PTSD, which leads to protective factors.

Q2. Define burnout and distinguish it from PTSD.

Burnout: chronic workplace stress with emotional exhaustion, cynicism or depersonalisation, and reduced personal accomplishment. An occupational phenomenon rather than a medical diagnosis. PTSD: a clinical diagnosis after a traumatic event, with intrusions, avoidance, negative changes in mood and thinking, and hyperarousal for more than a month. They co-occur: in a Korean paramedic study, post-traumatic stress was most strongly associated with burnout.

Q3. What is locus of control and why does it matter for paramedics?

A generalised belief about whether outcomes depend on one's own actions (internal) or on external forces (external). Internal orientation is associated with less burnout and post-traumatic stress in several occupational samples, and external with more. Paramedic practice requires separating what is in my control (process, preparation, response), my influence (team, culture, advocacy) and what I cannot control (outcomes, other people's behaviour). Caution: an inflated internal locus feeds guilt and moral injury, and some external attributions (systems) are accurate.

Q4. Define salutogenesis and sense of coherence, and name the three components.

Salutogenesis (Antonovsky) asks what creates and maintains health and why some people stay well despite stress, placing people on a health ease/dis-ease continuum. Sense of coherence is a global orientation to life that sees it as comprehensible (cognitive), manageable (behavioural, resources are available, including those of others) and meaningful (motivational). Strong SOC is associated with better mental health and less post-traumatic stress in paramedics and related workers (Streb 2014; Mantas-Jiménez 2024). General resistance resources help build it.

Q5. Outline a plan for maintaining your own health across a career.

Three levels: individual (sleep, food, activity, breathing and reflection, competence, connection, meaning, early help-seeking), team (peer support, check-ins, culture), organisational (rostering, psychosocial risk management, proactive support, leadership). Tie to SOC components, include an escalation plan, and acknowledge that evidence for some practices is modest.

Q6. A colleague seems burnt out. What do you do?

Ask, listen, encourage action, check in. Describe behaviours, not diagnoses. Ask about suicide if concerned. Know the escalation pathway and the limits of confidentiality, especially regarding patient safety.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Quoting a single PTSD prevalence figure without the range or source.
  • Using "burnout" and "PTSD" as interchangeable.
  • Saying "internal locus of control is always better" without discussing realism, systems and self-blame.
  • Describing SOC as "control". Salutogenesis does not treat control as central, and manageability includes resources held by others.
  • Treating the SOC score as a diagnostic label. There are no universal cut-offs.
  • Claiming SOC causes better health from cross-sectional studies.
  • Citing the TacMed blog as if it were a research study. Label it as practitioner opinion.
  • Listing only individual strategies. Always include team and organisational levels.
